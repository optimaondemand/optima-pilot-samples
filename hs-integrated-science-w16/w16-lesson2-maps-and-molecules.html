Where Organisms Live and What Their Molecules Say

Read forWhy similar climates do not hold similar species, and what islands show about where species come from.

The fossil record and comparative anatomy provide two lines of evidence for evolution that were available to nineteenth-century scientists. Two more lines of evidence have been developed since then, and they are equally powerful: the geographic distribution of species, and the molecular sequences inside every living cell. Neither of these lines of evidence was designed to test evolution. They were developed for other purposes and then found to point, independently, to the same evolutionary conclusion.

Biogeography

Biogeography is the study of the geographic distribution of species. If species were created independently and placed in the environments best suited to them, you would expect similar environments in different parts of the world to contain similar species. But that is not what the distribution of life on Earth shows. Australia, South America, and Africa have very different species even where their climates are similar. Marsupials (kangaroos, wombats, koalas) are found almost exclusively in Australia and a few in the Americas, even though placental mammals with similar ecological roles live everywhere else. The pattern makes sense if Australia was isolated from other landmasses early in mammalian history, allowing marsupials to diversify in the absence of placental competition.

Closer look · Biogeography: Where Organisms Live and Why

If species were created independently and placed in optimal environments, similar environments on different continents should contain similar species. But they do not. Australia's grasslands are populated by kangaroos and wallabies; the grasslands of Africa by zebras and antelope; the grasslands of North America by bison and pronghorn. These animals occupy similar ecological roles and live in similar climates, but they are not closely related. Each continent's fauna reflects the evolutionary history of the organisms that were present when the continent became isolated, not the climate the continent currently has.

Island biogeography provides some of the clearest examples. Islands close to a continent generally contain species closely related to those on the nearby mainland, even where the island has a different climate than other islands further away. The Galapagos Islands, located about 1,000 kilometers west of Ecuador, contain species found nowhere else on Earth, but those species are clearly related to South American species. The most famous example is the group of birds known as Darwin's finches: fourteen species found on the Galapagos, each with a differently shaped beak suited to a different food source, all descended from a single ancestral population that reached the islands from the South American mainland. The isolation of the islands allowed the ancestral population to diverge into many species over time.

If species were simply placed in the environments most suited to them, there would be no reason for island species to be most closely related to the nearby mainland rather than to species in similar climates elsewhere. The pattern of distribution tracks not climate but proximity and history — exactly what you would expect if species descended from ancestors that reached a location and then diversified.

Closer look · Island biogeography

Island biogeography provides especially clear examples. Islands colonized by a species from a nearby continent contain descendants of that ancestral population, not species perfectly matched to the island's climate from wherever such perfect species might exist. The birds of the Galapagos Islands are most closely related to birds of Ecuador, approximately 1,000 kilometers to the east, even though the Galapagos climate is drier than much of Ecuador. The island species are descended from mainland colonizers, not placed de novo for the island environment.

Closer look · Darwin's Finches: Evolution Observed in a Single Archipelago

Fourteen species of finch inhabit the Galapagos Islands, each with a beak shape specialized for a different food source: large crushing beaks for hard seeds, slender probing beaks for cactus nectar, sharp hooked beaks for insects, and so on. All fourteen are closely related to each other and to a single ancestral finch species from the South American mainland. The pattern reveals what happened: one colonizing population reached the islands, found diverse ecological opportunities unoccupied by competitors, and diversified over time as natural selection favored different beak shapes for different food sources on different islands. The result is a radiation of species from a single ancestor, all still bearing the clear molecular and structural signatures of their common origin.

Best Explanation

Cross-Discipline Connection: Scientific Method

The best explanation is the one that accounts for all the observations with the fewest assumptions. The distribution of life on Earth — marsupials in Australia, closely related island species to nearby mainlands, Darwin's finches all resembling each other more than they resemble birds elsewhere — is most simply explained by common descent and isolation. The alternative explanation would require a more complex set of assumptions about why independent creation placed related organisms in adjacent locations. Science selects the more parsimonious explanation: the one that does the most explanatory work with the least additional complexity.

Molecular Biology

Read forWhat every living organism shares, and how DNA sequences measure how closely two species are related.

The deepest evidence for evolution comes from the molecules inside living cells. Every living organism uses DNA to store genetic information, the same genetic code to translate that information into protein, and the same basic cellular machinery to carry out life's functions. This universal molecular toolkit is most simply explained by common descent: all life shares it because all life descends from a common ancestor that had it.

Beyond this universal framework, molecular biology provides a way to measure evolutionary relationships quantitatively. DNA sequences can be compared between species. Organisms that are more closely related should have more similar DNA sequences; organisms more distantly related should have more differences. When biologists compare DNA sequences across species, the resulting relationships match the picture drawn from fossils and comparative anatomy. Humans and chimpanzees share approximately 98.7% of their DNA sequences. Humans and mice share approximately 85%. Humans and fruit flies share around 60%. The closer the relationship inferred from anatomy and the fossil record, the more similar the DNA sequences.

Closer look · Molecular Biology: Reading Evolution in DNA

Every living organism carries DNA. Every DNA molecule accumulates mutations over time. These two facts make DNA an extraordinarily powerful record of evolutionary history.

When biologists compare DNA sequences between species, the pattern is consistent: the closer two species are in evolutionary terms, the more similar their DNA sequences. Humans and chimpanzees, which share a common ancestor approximately 6 million years ago, have DNA sequences that are approximately 98.7% identical. Humans and mice, which diverged from a common ancestor approximately 80 million years ago, share about 85% of their coding DNA sequences. Humans and fruit flies, separated by over 600 million years of evolution, still share approximately 60% of their functionally important genes. The decreasing similarity tracks the increasing time since divergence, exactly as evolutionary theory predicts.

The Molecular Clock

Read forWhat the clock counts, and why the dates it gives can be trusted.

A related technique, called the molecular clock, uses the fact that DNA accumulates mutations at a relatively steady rate over time. By comparing the number of differences between two species' DNA sequences, scientists can estimate how long ago those species shared a common ancestor, even without fossil evidence. Molecular clock estimates consistently align with dates inferred from the fossil record, providing a third independent confirmation of the evolutionary timeline.

Closer look · The Molecular Clock

DNA mutations accumulate at roughly predictable rates in different parts of the genome. Neutral mutations, which do not affect the function of the proteins they encode, accumulate at a particularly steady rate. This regularity allows scientists to use DNA differences as a clock: by comparing how different two species' DNA sequences are, they can estimate how long ago those species shared a common ancestor. This is the molecular clock.

Crucially, molecular clock estimates are independent of the fossil record. They are calculated from DNA sequences using estimated mutation rates, with no reference to when fossils appear in rock layers. Yet molecular clock dates consistently agree with dates derived from fossil evidence. When two completely independent methods, developed by different scientists for different purposes, converge on the same dates for evolutionary divergences, the agreement is powerful evidence that both methods are measuring the same real history.

Key Vocabulary

  • biogeography — the study of the geographic distribution of species and ecosystems across Earth
  • island biogeography — the study of how species colonize islands and diversify in isolation from mainland populations
  • molecular clock — the use of mutation rate to estimate the time elapsed since two species shared a common ancestor
  • DNA sequence comparison — comparing nucleotide sequences between species to measure their degree of relatedness
  • common descent — the principle that all living species share ancestors if you trace their lineages back far enough
  • divergence — the accumulation of differences between populations that were once part of the same species

History of Science: Twenty Years of Silence

Charles Darwin returned from his voyage on the HMS Beagle in 1836 with notebooks full of observations about species distributions and anatomical comparisons. By 1844 he had written a full account of his theory of natural selection. He showed it to no one and published nothing. He spent the next fourteen years methodically accumulating additional evidence, knowing that his conclusion would be controversial and that the case would need to be overwhelming.

In 1858, he received a letter from Alfred Russel Wallace, a naturalist working in the Malay Archipelago. Wallace had independently arrived at the same theory of natural selection and was asking Darwin to evaluate his manuscript before submitting it for publication. Darwin was stunned: the theory he had been carefully amassing for two decades had been reached independently by another man. The two papers were presented jointly to the Linnean Society of London in July 1858 — Darwin and Wallace announced the same discovery in the same year. Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859, the year after. Two men, working independently in different parts of the world, both studying species distributions and natural variation, arrived at identical conclusions. That convergence is itself evidence of a kind: when independent observations lead to the same conclusion, the conclusion is stronger.

Before moving on, think about: Molecular clock data and fossil record data are developed by completely different methods by scientists working in completely different fields. Yet they give the same estimates for when groups of organisms diverged from common ancestors. What does this convergence of independent lines of evidence tell you about the strength of the evolutionary conclusion?

The Long View · The Power of Independent Corroboration

Four lines of evidence, each developed by different scientists using different methods, all point to the same conclusion about the history of life. This is not what you would expect if the conclusion were wrong. Scientific theories that are mistaken tend to be exposed by exactly this kind of independent testing: the fossil record contradicts the geological timeline, or the molecular clock gives dates that don't match the anatomy, or the distribution of species doesn't match the predicted pattern. Evolution survives all of these tests because it is the best-supported conclusion available.

Independent Lines of Evidence: Biogeography and Molecular Biology

Overview

In science, when multiple independent lines of evidence point to the same conclusion, that conclusion becomes more reliable. This assignment asks you to work with two independent lines of evidence for evolution (biogeography and molecular biology) and explain what makes their agreement scientifically significant.
