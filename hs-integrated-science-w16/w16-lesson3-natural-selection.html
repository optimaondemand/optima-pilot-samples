The Logic of Natural Selection

The Logic of Natural Selection

Natural selection is not a force or a plan. It is an inevitable consequence of three simple facts: organisms vary, that variation is heritable, and some variants leave more offspring than others. Given those facts, evolution is not merely possible — it is unavoidable.

  • I will be able to explain why natural selection acts on phenotypes but evolutionary change occurs in allele frequencies.
  • I will be able to connect the mechanisms of natural selection to the evidence for evolution studied in the previous lessons.

The Mechanism of Natural Selection

Read forWhat each of the four conditions needs, and what happens when one is missing.

Evidence can establish that evolution has occurred. But a theory of evolution also needs to explain how it occurs. What process produces the changes documented in the fossil record and the DNA sequences? The answer is natural selection: a mechanism through which heritable variation in a population, combined with differences in survival and reproduction, produces changes in the population over generations.

Natural selection is not a plan or a force pushing organisms toward a goal. It is a consequence of simple conditions that, when present together, make evolutionary change inevitable.

The Four Required Conditions

For natural selection to occur, four conditions must be present simultaneously. If any one is absent, natural selection cannot operate.

1. Variation. Individuals in a population must differ from one another in some heritable trait. If all individuals were identical, there would be nothing for selection to act on. Variation exists because of mutation, genetic recombination during meiosis, and gene flow between populations.

Closer look · Variation: The Raw Material

Individuals in any population differ from each other in countless ways: body size, coloration, behavioral tendencies, biochemical properties, disease resistance. This variation exists because of mutation (random changes in DNA sequences), genetic recombination during meiosis (the reshuffling of alleles), and gene flow (the movement of alleles between populations). Without variation, there is nothing for selection to act on; every individual would have equal fitness and the population would remain genetically static.

2. Heritability. The variation must be heritable, meaning it must be passed from parents to offspring. A trait that results entirely from an individual's experience or environment (a scar, for example) is not heritable and cannot be selected for. Heritability requires that the variation has a genetic basis.

Closer look · Heritability: The Transmission Requirement

Not all differences between individuals are heritable. A scar from an injury, a change in fur color due to diet, a behavioral habit learned from experience — none of these are genetically transmitted to offspring. Natural selection can only act on differences that are encoded in DNA and passed from parent to offspring through gametes.

3. Differential survival and reproduction. Individuals with different variants must differ in their ability to survive and reproduce in their environment. If all variants were equally likely to survive and reproduce, selection would produce no directional change. The environment acts as a filter: some variants allow their bearers to survive longer and leave more offspring; others do not.

Closer look · Differential Reproduction: The Filtering Mechanism

If all variants reproduce equally well, the population's allele frequencies will remain stable (or change only by random drift). Selection requires that some variants, on average, leave more offspring than others. This difference can come from survival (some individuals live longer, leaving more reproduction opportunities), from reproductive rate (some individuals produce more offspring per unit time), or from offspring survival (some individuals' offspring are more likely to survive to reproduce themselves). The environment acts as the filter: it determines which variants survive and reproduce.

4. A population that is not too small. In very small populations, random chance events can overwhelm the effects of selection. This phenomenon, called genetic drift, can cause allele frequencies to change in ways unrelated to fitness. Natural selection operates most predictably in populations large enough that random variation in survival and reproduction averages out.

Two Men, One Theory

History of Science: Two Men, One Theory, One Year

The theory of natural selection was not one person's idea. By 1858, Charles Darwin had accumulated twenty years of evidence and written a full account of his theory, but had published nothing. Alfred Russel Wallace, working in the Malay Archipelago, independently arrived at the same theory and wrote it up in two days during a fever. His letter to Darwin prompted Darwin to finally act.

On July 1, 1858, papers by both men were read before the Linnean Society of London. Darwin's contribution was read in absentia while he was absent from London, grief-stricken after the death of his young son. Neither paper caused immediate public reaction. It was the publication of On the Origin of Species in 1859 that transformed the scientific and cultural landscape. Darwin received most of the credit, partly because his book was far more detailed. Wallace was generous about this. He later wrote that Darwin deserved the priority because of the depth of his evidence. The convergence of two independent minds on the same theory, independently, is itself a piece of evidence: when the same logic follows inevitably from the same observations, the conclusion tends to be right.

Three Types of Natural Selection

Read forWhich part of a trait's range each type favors, and what happens to the population average.

Selection does not always push a population in the same direction. Three patterns of selection describe how different conditions produce different outcomes.

Directional selection occurs when one extreme of a trait's range is favored over the other. Over time, the population average shifts in the direction of the favored extreme. An example is antibiotic resistance in bacteria: random mutations occasionally produce bacteria that can survive antibiotic exposure. When an antibiotic is applied, bacteria without the resistance allele die; bacteria with it survive and reproduce. Over many generations, the population becomes dominated by resistant individuals. The environment (the antibiotic) has directed selection toward one extreme.

Closer look · Directional Selection

When one extreme of a trait's range consistently outreproduces the other, the population mean shifts over time toward the favored extreme. Antibiotic resistance is directional selection at high speed: bacteria with resistance alleles survive antibiotic exposure and reproduce; bacteria without them die. Each generation, the frequency of resistance alleles increases. Over enough generations, the population becomes dominated by resistant bacteria. Antibiotic resistance is natural selection in real time, observable within months or years in a hospital setting. It is one of the reasons understanding evolution is not merely academic: it is a medical and public health reality.

Stabilizing selection occurs when intermediate values of a trait are favored over either extreme. Human birth weight is a classic example: very low birth weight and very high birth weight are both associated with higher infant mortality, so selection has maintained a distribution centered around an intermediate weight. Stabilizing selection does not produce dramatic change; it maintains the status quo by eliminating extreme variants.

Closer look · Stabilizing Selection

When the current population mean is already well-suited to the environment, selection often acts against extremes in both directions, maintaining the current mean. Human birth weight shows stabilizing selection: very low birth weight increases infant mortality due to physiological immaturity; very high birth weight increases mortality due to delivery complications. Selection has thus maintained a distribution centered around an intermediate range over human evolutionary history. Stabilizing selection does not produce dramatic change; it is the evolutionary force of conservation, maintaining what works.

Disruptive selection occurs when both extremes of a trait are favored over the intermediate. This is the least common type but can eventually lead to the formation of two distinct groups within a population. An example is a population of birds feeding on seeds of two very different sizes: birds with small beaks are efficient at small seeds, birds with large beaks are efficient at large seeds, and intermediate-beaked birds are efficient at neither. Over time, the population may split into two groups, each specialized for one food source.

Closer look · Disruptive Selection

When two different niches in the same environment favor opposite extremes of a trait, disruptive selection can split a population into two distinct groups. The African finch Pyrenestes ostrinus provides an example: some individuals have large beaks suited for hard seeds; others have small beaks suited for soft seeds. Intermediate beak sizes are less efficient at either food type and are disfavored. Disruptive selection, sustained over time, can contribute to the formation of two distinct populations — eventually, perhaps, two distinct species.

Words of the Mechanism

Key Vocabulary

  • natural selection — the process by which heritable traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common in a population over generations
  • heritability — the degree to which a trait is genetically transmitted from parent to offspring
  • directional selection — selection that favors one extreme of a trait's range, shifting the population mean over time
  • stabilizing selection — selection that favors intermediate trait values and eliminates extremes, maintaining the current mean
  • disruptive selection — selection that favors both extremes of a trait while eliminating intermediate values
  • fitness — the reproductive success of an individual relative to others in the same population; how well-suited an organism is to its environment

Pause and Reflect

Cross-Discipline Connection: Genetics to Evolution

Natural selection acts on phenotypes — the visible, functional traits of organisms. But the change that accumulates over evolutionary time is a change in allele frequencies. The mutations are the source of the new variation that natural selection can act on. The mechanism of meiosis, including crossing over and independent assortment, constantly reshuffles existing alleles into new combinations, creating the variation on which selection acts each generation. Without the genetic machinery, natural selection would have no raw material to work with. Evolution is impossible without the genetics that produces heritable variation.

A farmer uses a pesticide that kills 95% of an insect pest population. After a few years, the pesticide no longer works effectively. Using your knowledge of natural selection, explain what most likely happened.

Before moving on, think about: Natural selection has no goal and no direction of its own. It simply favors whatever variants happen to reproduce more successfully in a given environment. What does this mean for the word "progress" in evolution? Is evolution making organisms better in any absolute sense, or only better suited to a particular environment at a particular time?

The Long View · Selection Acts on Phenotypes; Evolution Changes Allele Frequencies

One of the most important subtleties in evolutionary biology is the relationship between what selection sees and what changes over time. Selection acts on phenotypes: a predator eats the moth it can see, not the allele the moth carries. But evolutionary change is measured in allele frequencies: over generations, the dark allele becomes more or less common depending on which phenotype is more likely to survive. The individual is selected; the population evolves. No individual organism evolves during its lifetime. Evolution is a population-level phenomenon that operates across generations, through the differential survival and reproduction of individuals carrying different alleles.

Hospitals have tracked the average weight of newborn babies over the past 100 years and found that very low-weight and very high-weight babies have higher mortality rates, while mid-range babies survive at the highest rate. The distribution of birth weights has remained relatively stable.
