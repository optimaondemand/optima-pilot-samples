What Bones and Stone Tell Us About Life's History

The evidence for evolution was not invented by one person or discovered in a single moment. It has accumulated across two centuries, from four independent directions. This lesson examines the first two: what fossils say and what comparing bodies reveals.

  • I will be able to evaluate why the incompleteness of the fossil record does not undermine evolutionary theory.
  • I will be able to apply the logic of comparative anatomy to infer evolutionary relationships between species.

The Case Built in Stone and Bone

Read forWhy an incomplete fossil record still shows a clear pattern, and what makes a fossil “transitional.”

If evolution is true — if species have changed over time and all living things share common ancestors — then it should leave evidence behind. The history of life should be readable, at least in part, from the record that organisms have left in rock, in their bodies, and in their genes. It turns out that record exists, and it is extensive. No single piece of evidence would be enough to establish evolution as the foundational framework of biology. But four independent lines of evidence, each developed by different scientists using different methods, all point to the same conclusion. This reading examines the first two: the fossil record and comparative anatomy.

The Fossil Record

A fossil is any preserved trace of an organism that lived in the past. Fossils form in several ways: hard parts such as bones and shells can be replaced by minerals in a process called permineralization; an organism can be trapped in amber; soft tissues can leave impressions in fine sediment. The vast majority of organisms that have ever lived left no fossil at all. Fossilization requires a precise set of circumstances, and soft-bodied organisms in environments that erode rather than deposit are rarely preserved. The fossil record is therefore incomplete by nature.

Even so, it is not random. Sedimentary rock layers, called strata, deposit in sequence: older layers below, newer layers above. By examining fossils at different depths and in different strata across the world, paleontologists have assembled a picture of how life changed through time. The pattern is consistent: simple, single-celled organisms appear in the oldest rocks; complex multicellular organisms appear later; the major animal groups appear in a predictable sequence. Fish precede amphibians, which precede reptiles, which precede mammals and birds. This sequence has never been violated. No rabbit has ever been found in Cambrian rock.

Closer look · Reading the Fossil Record

Sedimentary rock forms in layers, with older deposits beneath newer ones. Organisms preserved in those layers — as mineralized bone, shell, impression, or amber — provide a record of what lived at different points in Earth's past. That record is incomplete: most organisms never fossilize, and most fossils are destroyed by erosion or geological pressure before they are found. Yet the record that remains is consistent, detailed, and unambiguous in its pattern.

The pattern is this: organisms in older rocks are consistently simpler and more different from living species than organisms in younger rocks. The sequence of major groups through time is predictable: bacteria appear before eukaryotes, invertebrates before vertebrates, fish before amphibians, reptiles before mammals and birds. This sequence holds everywhere in the world. No rabbit fossil has been found in Cambrian rock. No modern whale has been found below the layer where the earliest whale ancestors appear. The succession of life through deep time is ordered and directional.

Transitional fossils are particularly compelling. A transitional fossil is an organism with features intermediate between two groups, documenting a step in an evolutionary lineage. One famous example is Tiktaalik, a fish-like organism from approximately 375 million years ago that possessed fins with an internal skeletal structure resembling a wrist, and a neck capable of independent movement from its body. These are features found in four-limbed land vertebrates (tetrapods) but not in ordinary fish. Tiktaalik was discovered precisely where evolutionary theory predicted it would be found: in rocks of the right age to represent the fish-to-tetrapod transition. Another example is Archaeopteryx, which has feathers and a wishbone like a modern bird, but also teeth, a long bony tail, and clawed fingers on its wings like a small dinosaur. These organisms are not "missing links" in the sense of unexplained gaps; they are documented stages in evolutionary lineages.

Closer look · Transitional Fossils: Documents of Change in Progress

Tiktaalik, discovered in Arctic Canada in rocks approximately 375 million years old, was a fish with a difference. Its fins had an internal skeletal structure — a humerus, radius, and ulna — that corresponds to the limb bones of four-legged land vertebrates. It also had a neck, capable of turning independently of its body, a feature absent in fish but present in tetrapods. Tiktaalik was not a fish becoming a tetrapod in a single generation; it was a member of a population living at the evolutionary transition between aquatic and terrestrial life. Its discovery was predicted by evolutionary theory before the fossil was found: scientists knew approximately where in time the fish-to-tetrapod transition should be and searched rocks of that age in locations likely to have preserved freshwater shallow-water organisms.

Archaeopteryx, from approximately 150 million years ago, has feathers, a wishbone, and a backward-pointing toe like modern birds. It also has teeth (no living bird has teeth), a long bony tail, and three clawed fingers on each wing, like the small theropod dinosaurs from which birds evolved. It is neither simply "a dinosaur" nor simply "a bird." It is an organism that was alive when the lineage leading to modern birds was diverging from the dinosaurs.

Comparative Anatomy

Read forThe difference between a homologous structure and a vestigial one, and what each tells you about the past.

Comparative anatomy is the study of structural similarities and differences among organisms. Two types of structural similarity are especially informative about evolutionary history.

Homologous structures are body parts that share a common evolutionary origin, even if they now serve different functions. The forelimb of a human, the flipper of a whale, the wing of a bat, and the front leg of a dog all share the same underlying bone arrangement: one proximal bone, two distal bones, then a group of smaller bones, then digits. These structures are built on the same blueprint. The best explanation is that all four mammals inherited this basic limb structure from a common ancestor, and that natural selection has modified it differently in each lineage to suit different functions. Common descent with modification is the most parsimonious explanation for why four very different animals share an identical underlying architecture.

Closer look · Homologous Structures

Homologous structures are body parts that share a common evolutionary origin even though they may serve different functions in different species. The forelimbs of vertebrates provide the clearest example. A human arm, a whale's flipper, a bat's wing, and a horse's front leg look very different externally and perform very different functions. Yet they all share the same underlying skeletal arrangement: one bone (the humerus), connected to two bones (radius and ulna), connected to a cluster of smaller bones (carpals), connected to elongated digits.

This shared blueprint cannot be explained by functional optimization alone. A flipper optimized for swimming would not be built from a wrist and five digits. A wing optimized for flying does not require a radius and ulna. These structures are shared because they were inherited from a common ancestor, then modified by natural selection in each lineage toward the different functions they now serve. The unity of the blueprint beneath the diversity of function is the signature of descent with modification.

Vestigial structures are body parts that have been reduced in size or function over evolutionary time but can still be identified in the organism's anatomy. The human coccyx (tailbone) consists of several fused vertebrae at the base of the spine. In our ancestors, this structure was part of a functional tail used for balance and communication. In modern humans it is a small remnant, retained because natural selection has not acted strongly enough to eliminate it entirely. Whales retain pelvic bones embedded in their musculature, the remains of hind limbs from the land-dwelling ancestors of whales. Snakes retain small, nonfunctional vestiges of leg bones in some species. These are not scars or errors; they are historical residue, carrying the record of the past inside the bodies of living organisms.

Closer look · Vestigial Structures

Vestigial structures are anatomical features reduced in size or function over evolutionary time — remnants of structures that served clear functions in an ancestor. The human coccyx is a series of fused vertebrae at the base of the spine. In our primate ancestors, this structure was a functional tail used for balance and social communication. In modern humans it is much reduced, but its presence in human anatomy makes sense only in light of evolutionary history. Similarly, some snake species retain small, nonfunctional pelvic bones and vestigial leg structures embedded in their musculature — relics of the legs their lizard-like ancestors possessed. Whales retain pelvic bones embedded in their body wall, remnants of hind limbs from the terrestrial ancestors of whales. These structures are not errors or accidents; they are evolutionary history made visible inside living organisms.

Science Now

Contemporary Research: Ancient DNA and Paleogenomics

The discovery that DNA can be preserved in ancient bone and permafrost has opened a new line of evolutionary evidence that nineteenth-century scientists could not have imagined. Researchers have now sequenced the complete genomes of Neanderthals, Denisovans (an archaic human population known from a single finger bone), and woolly mammoths, among others. These ancient genomes allow scientists to test evolutionary hypotheses directly at the molecular level. We can now confirm that modern humans and Neanderthals interbred, and that roughly 1-4% of the DNA of non-African modern humans comes from Neanderthal ancestors. Ancient DNA research is accelerating rapidly as techniques improve: sequences that were considered irretrievable from specimens just ten years ago are now routinely analyzed. This field did not exist as recently as 1990.

Comparative Embryology

Read forWhat embryos share early on, what they become later, and the old claim scientists now reject.

Comparative embryology is the study of how the embryos of different species develop and how those stages compare. It is the developmental side of comparative anatomy: the same logic of shared structure, applied to embryos instead of adult bodies. Scientists count it with the anatomical line of evidence, alongside fossils, biogeography, and molecular biology. Early in development, the embryos of fish, chickens, and humans look strikingly alike. All of them form pharyngeal arches and pouches along the throat region, and all of them have a tail that extends past the anus, called a post-anal tail. As development continues, the embryos diverge. In fish the arches become parts of the gills and jaw. In humans they become structures of the jaw, the middle ear, and the throat, and the tail shrinks until only the coccyx remains.

The best explanation is common ancestry. Vertebrates inherited the same basic developmental instructions from a shared ancestor, including families of regulatory genes such as the Hox genes, which help set up the body plan from head to tail. Different lineages then modified those instructions at later stages. This fits what the fossil record and comparative anatomy already show: the same pattern of shared structure beneath different forms.

Be careful about what this does and does not mean. A human embryo does not pass through an adult fish stage, and its throat arches are not working gills. These structures are retained developmental pathways. In the nineteenth century, Ernst Haeckel claimed that “ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny,” meaning that an embryo replays its species' whole evolutionary history. That claim was overstated, and scientists reject it. The modern claim is narrower and better supported: related species share early developmental patterns because they share ancestry. Embryology is strongest when it is combined with fossils, anatomy, and the molecular evidence you will meet next.

Key Vocabulary

  • fossil record — the accumulated body of fossils preserved in rock strata, representing organisms that lived in the past
  • transitional fossil — a fossil with features intermediate between an ancestral group and its evolutionary descendants
  • comparative anatomy — the study of structural similarities and differences among species
  • homologous structures — body parts in different species that share a common evolutionary origin but may have different functions
  • vestigial structures — reduced or nonfunctional body parts that are remnants of structures that were functional in ancestors
  • strata — layers of sedimentary rock, deposited over time; older layers are typically deeper

Courage and Evidence

History of Science: Lamarck's Courageous Mistake

Jean-Baptiste de Lamarck, a French naturalist, published his theory of evolution in 1809 — fifty years before Darwin. He was the first scientist to propose that species change over time, a radical claim at a moment when most naturalists held that species were fixed and separately created. He was right about the fact of evolution. He was wrong about the mechanism.

Lamarck believed that organisms could pass on traits they had acquired during their lifetimes. A giraffe that stretched its neck to reach higher leaves would, he thought, produce offspring with slightly longer necks. We now know this is not how inheritance works. Traits acquired by an individual organism are not passed to offspring; only heritable genetic differences are. But Lamarck's willingness to follow the evidence of change in the fossil record, even when it contradicted the received view, required the kind of intellectual courage that all genuine scientific advance demands. History has remembered his mechanism as wrong but rarely pauses to note that his basic claim about evolution was correct half a century before it was proven.

Virtue Focus: Courage in the Face of Evidence

Lamarck was wrong about the mechanism of inheritance, but he was courageous enough to follow the fossil evidence to the conclusion that species change over time — a conclusion that contradicted the scientific consensus and the cultural assumptions of his era. Darwin accumulated twenty years of evidence before he published, knowing his conclusion would be controversial. The courage this module asks of students is the same: the willingness to follow the evidence where it leads, even when the conclusion is unfamiliar or uncomfortable. This is not recklessness. It is the opposite of recklessness. It is the discipline of letting the world tell you what it is, rather than telling the world what it should be.

Before moving on, think about: A human arm and a whale flipper perform completely different functions. Why would a biologist argue that their identical underlying bone arrangement is evidence for evolution rather than simply evidence that both work well for their purpose? What would it take to show that the similarity is due to common descent rather than a shared engineering solution?

The Long View · Why Four Lines of Evidence Matter

This lesson examined two of the four major lines of evidence for evolution. The power of the evolutionary case does not rest on any single line of evidence. It rests on the convergence of four independent lines, developed by different scientists using different methods, all arriving at the same conclusion.
