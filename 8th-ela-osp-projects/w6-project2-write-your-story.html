Five rules for a first draft
  1. Stepping stones. Write one beat of your outline at a time.
  2. Senses first. Your reader discovers the place as your traveler does.
  3. One point of view. Stay behind your traveler’s eyes the whole way.
  4. Mark the speech. Type [SPEECH] where it goes, and keep writing.
  5. Polish later. A finished rough draft beats a perfect first paragraph.

Hear the voice first

Open your copy to Chapter 20 and read one paragraph of the Oyarsa’s judgment aloud. Notice:

  • Long sentences that gather two or three ideas before they rest, so a short sentence lands hard.
  • One exact image instead of a list of adjectives.
  • Slightly formal words, never casual modern speech.
  • Wonder and judgment together.
1. Who speaks, to whom, and why now?
2. The one thing this character most needs to say
3. One exact detail they notice while they speak (one only)
4. The opening sentence, long enough to set a slow rhythm
5. The closing line, short and final, like a decision
📌 Watch how Jonah wrote Tess’s speech
Tess, to the pillars
Jonah · 8th Grade Scholar

I came here to put you on a map, and I thought that was the whole of my errand: forty marks on a ridge, a column of numbers, and a long sleep home. I called you machines because machines were the only word I had, and a word is easier to carry than a question. I took a piece of you to prove it. You did not stop me. You only went on looking at the dark edge of the sky, as patient as the light under the ice. Then I hummed, and you answered, and I understood that I was the one who had not been listening. I do not know your language. Perhaps I never shall. But I know now that a thing can be very old and very quiet and still be alive. When I draw this map again, I will not draw you as a place. Tell me what you are waiting for. I will wait with you.

Notice: the long first sentence gathers three things; “You did not stop me” lands short. One image (the light under the ice), not a list.
