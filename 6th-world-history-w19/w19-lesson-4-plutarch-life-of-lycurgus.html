A Writer, a Lawgiver, and Iron Money

Today's Focus

How far can we trust what Plutarch says about Lycurgus?

Plutarch wrote about the Spartan lawgiver many hundreds of years after his time. You will read one passage about Sparta's iron money, set it beside the translator's words, and decide what a careful reader can trust.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…
  1. tell who wrote the passage, and how long after Lycurgus.
  2. compare the plain version with the translator's words.
  3. explain Plutarch's reasoning about iron money.
  4. separate what you can reasonably trust from what you should question.

Chapter 1

About the Writer

About this source: Plutarch was a Greek writer who lived from about AD 46 to about AD 120. He wrote biographies of famous Greeks and Romans, including Lycurgus, the lawgiver Sparta credited with its way of life. Plutarch wrote many hundreds of years after the time Lycurgus would have lived, and he admits that his sources disagree. The passage below is adapted from a public-domain translation.

What would you do with money that was too heavy to carry? A Greek writer says Sparta chose it on purpose. Ask yourself what the Spartans wanted to stop, and what they gave up.

You have learned that Sparta closed itself off from the rest of Greece. One part of that closed world was its money. Here is how Plutarch describes it.

As you read, remember that this is a Roman-era writer describing a Spartan lawgiver who is half legend. A good reader asks both what the passage says and how far it can be trusted.

Chapter 2

The Passage

Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus, chapter 9 (adapted from a public-domain translation). Plutarch wrote many hundreds of years after the time of Lycurgus.

Lycurgus made a rule that only iron money should be used. He gave it a small value for its great weight, so that a sum of ten minas needed a large storeroom in the house and a yoke of oxen to carry it.

When this money became the custom, many kinds of wrongdoing left Sparta. For who would steal it, or take it as a bribe, or rob for it, when it could not be hidden, could not be enjoyed, and could not even be cut up to any profit?

Chapter 3

In the Translator's Words

Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus, chapter 9, sections 1 and 2. Translated by Bernadotte Perrin, Loeb Classical Library, 1914, as printed by LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Lycurgus*.html). Spelling and punctuation are the translator's. The sentence that follows is cut, shown as · · ·.

Next, he undertook to divide up their movable property also, in order that every vestige of unevenness and inequality might be removed; and when he saw that they could not bear to have it taken from them directly, he took another course, and overcame their avarice by political devices. In the first place, he withdrew all gold and silver money from currency, and ordained the use of iron money only. Then to a great weight and mass of this he gave a trifling value, so that ten minas' worth required a large store-room in the house, and a yoke of cattle to transport it.

When this money obtained currency, many sorts of iniquity went into exile from Lacedaemon. For who would steal, or receive as a bribe, or rob, or plunder that which could neither be concealed, nor possessed with satisfaction, nay, nor even cut to pieces with any profit?

As You Read, Notice

Plutarch tells us what Lycurgus wanted the money to be like. Find the words that describe it.

Look at the last sentence, which is a question. What kinds of wrongdoing does Plutarch name?

Plutarch gives no date and names no earlier writer for this passage. Keep that in mind.
