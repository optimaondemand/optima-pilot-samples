A Farmer, a Brother, and Two Strifes

Today's Focus

What does a farmer with a grievance think keeps a city going?

Hesiod wrote about his own quarrel and ended up asking what makes a city last. You will read two short passages in a plain version and in a translator's words, and weigh what they can tell us.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…
  1. tell who wrote Works and Days, when, and to whom.
  2. explain the two kinds of Strife that Hesiod describes.
  3. explain what Hesiod says happens to a city with fair judges and to a city with crooked ones.
  4. compare a plain version of a passage with a translator's words.

Question 1 · Two Kinds of Strife

Hesiod says there are two kinds of Strife, which is the Greek idea of contest. Using what you have learned, explain how Hesiod's good Strife differs from the contest Homer's heroes pursued, and why a city might want one kind and fear the other.

Question 2 · Straight Judgments

Imagine that the judges of a Greek polis began taking gifts from rich men and ruling in their favor. Using Hesiod's words and what you know about his quarrel with his brother, explain what Hesiod would expect to happen to that city, and what an ordinary farmer might do. Use what you have learned to support your answer.

Question 3 · Your Verdict

Hesiod believes a city lives or dies by whether its judges are fair. Do you agree? Take a side, and defend it using Hesiod and what you have learned. Say what the strongest argument against your view would be.

Chapter 1

About the Poet

About this source: Hesiod was a farmer and poet who lived in central Greece around 700 BC. Works and Days is a long poem addressed to his brother Perses, with whom he quarreled over family land. It mixes advice about farming with warnings about justice. Hesiod wrote in Greek verse. The passages below are in plain prose, adapted from a public-domain translation, so the wording is close but not exact.

A farmer with a grievance against his own brother sat down to write a poem, and it ended up asking what makes a city last. Listen for what a man who works with his hands thinks matters more than glory.

You have met two ideas of what makes a person admirable: the hero who wins glory, and the good neighbor who deals fairly. Here the second idea speaks for itself, in the voice of a working farmer.

Read each excerpt slowly. The first is about a word, Strife. The second is about judges and what happens to a city when they do their work well or badly.

Chapter 2

Two Kinds of Strife

Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 11 to 24 (adapted from a public-domain translation)

So there was never just one kind of Strife. There are two on the earth. One a man would praise when he understood her, and the other he would blame, for they have quite different hearts. One makes ugly war and fighting grow. She is cruel, and no man loves her, but men honor this heavy Strife because the gods require it.

The other is the elder daughter of dark Night. She stirs even the helpless man to work. A man grows eager for work when he looks at his neighbor, a rich man who hurries to plant and sow and put his house in good order. Neighbor competes with neighbor as he hurries after wealth. This Strife is good for men.

Chapter 3

In the Translator's Words

Hugh G. Evelyn-White, translator, Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns and Homerica (Loeb Classical Library, 1914), Works and Days, lines 11 to 24. Project Gutenberg #348, gutenberg.org/ebooks/348. Spelling and punctuation are the translator's. This one paragraph is shown in two parts.

So, after all, there was not one kind of Strife alone, but all over the earth there are two. As for the one, a man would praise her when he came to understand her; but the other is blameworthy: and they are wholly different in nature. For one fosters evil war and battle, being cruel: her no man loves; but perforce, through the will of the deathless gods, men pay harsh Strife her honour due.

But the other is the elder daughter of dark Night, and the son of Cronos who sits above and dwells in the aether, set her in the roots of the earth: and she is far kinder to men. She stirs up even the shiftless to toil; for a man grows eager to work when he considers his neighbour, a rich man who hastens to plough and plant and put his house in good order; and neighbour vies with his neighbour as he hurries after wealth. This Strife is wholesome for men. And potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel.

Chapter 4

Straight Judgments

Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 225 to 231 and 240 to 241 (adapted from a public-domain translation)

But those who give straight judgments to strangers and to their own people, and never turn aside from what is just, their city blooms, and the people in it flourish. Famine never keeps company with men who judge straight.

But for those who practice violence and cruel deeds, Zeus, who sees from far away, appoints a punishment. Often even a whole city suffers because of one bad man.

Chapter 5

In the Translator's Words

Evelyn-White translation (1914), Works and Days, lines 225 to 231 and 238 to 241. Project Gutenberg #348. Spelling and punctuation are the translator's. A · · · marks lines left out.

But they who give straight judgements to strangers and to the men of the land, and go not aside from what is just, their city flourishes, and the people prosper in it: Peace, the nurse of children, is abroad in their land, and all-seeing Zeus never decrees cruel war against them. Neither famine nor disaster ever haunt men who do true justice; but light-heartedly they tend the fields which are all their care.
