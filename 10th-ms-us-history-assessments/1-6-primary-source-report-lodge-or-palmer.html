Your Five Steps

Write in complete paragraphs. You do not need a title page. Cite the source, the lesson, and your JStor article by name.

1
Place the source. Who wrote it, when, and for what audience? What was the author trying to get readers to do or believe?
2
State the main claim in your own words.
3
Point to evidence. Quote or describe one specific passage the author uses to support the claim.
4
Test the claim. Using the lesson “The League Rejected, 1919 to 1922” or the lesson “Red Scare, Strikes, and the Closing Door,” and one scholarly article you find on JStor, say what they confirm, what they complicate, and what the author leaves out.
5
Name a limit. Tell one thing you cannot learn about this period from this source alone.
Research Desk · JStor
  • It holds: Journal articles, books, and primary sources in one searchable library.
  • You get in: Your teacher posts the school's JStor login for this assignment. The login is not on the project page.
  • You want: One historian's argument about your source's subject, not a summary of events.

1. Search

Start from your source. Put an exact phrase in quotation marks and join terms with AND.

In Advanced Search, each term gets its own box, with AND, OR, or NOT between them. Below the boxes, narrow the results by item type and publication date.

A search for “League of Nations” alone returns thousands of results. Decide what the best next move is before you open anything.

2. Read before you save

Open a result and read the abstract or the first-page preview. Ask five questions.

  • Who wrote it? The author's name is under the title, on the results list and on the first page.
  • When was it published? The year is in the line that names the journal, its volume, and its issue.
  • Where was it published? A scholarly journal appears with a volume, an issue, and page numbers. Book reviews and front matter are not articles.
  • What does it argue? Find the sentence in the abstract or the first page that states the author's argument. Put it in your own words.
  • Does it speak to your source? Look for your author, the event, or the years 1919 and 1920 in the abstract. An article about a different decade will not test your claim.
AI tools can invent articles and authors that do not exist. Use only an article you opened on JStor yourself.

3. Cite

Every item on JStor has a Cite button that gives the citation in several styles. Copy the Chicago citation.

Pattern, not a real article:
Last, First. “Title of the Article.” Journal Name vol. #, no. # (Year): #–#. https://www.jstor.org/stable/#####.

4. Keep three notes

The project book gathers these with your other prep:

  • Your search. Terms you used, results, what you changed.
  • Your article. Copied from JStor's Cite button.
  • What it argues. Its argument in one sentence, and whether it confirms or complicates your source.
