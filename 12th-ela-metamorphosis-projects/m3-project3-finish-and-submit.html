Reverse Outline

Open your draft. For each section, scene, segment, round, or entry, write what it does for the claim in one line. Use the cards as a guide; add lines in your own file if you have more sections.

1The opening
The job: puts the claim and the question in front of the reader.
2First middle section
The job, in one line, and the text or source it uses.
3The counter-view
Who holds it, and how you answer it.
4The ending
What the reader holds that they did not hold at the start.

Read your lines from top to bottom. If two sections do the same job, merge them. If a section has no job, cut it or give it one.

D

Working with AI

What it can and cannot do
  • An AI tool can flag grammar and punctuation and suggest alternative wordings.
  • It can produce fluent sentences that misstate a text, and citations for articles that do not exist. It has not read your copy of the novella or the JStor article you saved.
  • It does not know what you think. The claim, every quotation, and every citation in your project are yours to check against the text and the source.
  • Using one is not required. If your teacher has a policy on it, follow that policy. If you used one, say how in your process record.

Four Entries You Need

MLA 9 builds each entry from the same parts: author, title, container, other contributors, version, publisher, date, location. Match each source you used to one of these four patterns.

1A translated book
The Metamorphosis, Meditations

Last, First. Title of Book. Translated by First Last, Publisher, Year.

Credit the translator after the title. Use the year of your edition, not the year of the original.

In the text (Last #), or (Last, pt. #) if your copy has no page numbers.

2A story or poem in an anthology
“Miss Brill”, “Araby”, “The Collar”, “The Tyger”

Last, First. “Title of Work.” Title of Anthology, edited by First Last, Publisher, Year, pp. #–#.

The short work takes quotation marks. The book that holds it takes italics.

In the text (Last #) for prose, (Last, line #) for verse.

3An online public-domain text
Self-Reliance, Dickinson, Blake

Last, First. “Title of Work.” Title of Book or Collection, Year of original. Name of Site, www.address.org/#####.

Copy the web address without https://. Cite the site you actually read from, not the first one you found.

In the text (Last) when the site has no page numbers.

4A JStor journal article
Your two Research Desk sources

Last, First. “Title of Article.” Journal Name, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. #–#. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/#####.

The journal is the first container and JSTOR is the second. Two authors: Last, First, and First Last. Three or more: Last, First, et al.

In the text (Last #), with the page where the idea sits.

Using JStor’s Cite button

The Cite button on the article page gives an MLA entry. Copy it, then check it against pattern 4: the article title in quotation marks, the journal in italics, JSTOR as the second container, and the stable address. Your teacher posts the school’s JStor login in the Module 3 Project item in Canvas.

The List Itself

  • One entry for the translation of The Metamorphosis I used, each other module text I used, and each of my two JStor sources.
  • Every source I cite in the project has an entry, and every entry is cited somewhere in the project.
  • Entries run in alphabetical order by the first word, ignoring A, An, and The.
  • The list is headed Works Cited, double-spaced, with a hanging indent: the first line of each entry at the margin, the rest indented half an inch.
  • Each entry has its container, its date, and a web address or JSTOR location where one exists.
  • Spoken and film formats: the Works Cited is a separate document, uploaded with the recording, and the sources are named aloud or on screen where I use them.

Spoken and Recorded Work

For a podcast, an oration, a recorded debate, or a film. Run it start to finish before the final take.

Your format
  • Pace. I timed a full run and it fits the length in the craft standard for my format. I slowed down for the sentence that carries the claim.
  • Emphasis. In each section I marked the words that carry the point, and I lean on them.
  • One passage aloud. I chose the passage from the text that matters most, and I read it slowly, once, with the pauses its punctuation asks for.
  • Audience. I know who is listening or watching and what that person needs in the first thirty seconds.
Recording
  • Record in a quiet room with soft surfaces, such as a closet of clothes or a room with curtains and a rug.
  • Hold a phone a hand’s width from your mouth, a little to the side, to soften breath sounds.
  • Record ten seconds and play them back with headphones before the real take.
  • For film, check the sound before the picture. Viewers forgive a plain shot sooner than a muffled line.
  • Keep the original file. Export a copy to upload.
B

Written Work

For an essay, letters or a diary, a written debate, or a screenplay. Read the whole thing aloud once, at speaking pace.

Your format
  • I read it aloud from the first line to the last, without stopping to edit.
  • I marked every stumble: a sentence I ran out of breath on, a repeated word, a quotation whose frame does not read cleanly.
  • I fixed each mark, then read the fixed paragraph aloud again.
  • I checked each quotation: the frame before it, the words themselves, and the explanation after it.

C

Craft by Format

Format
The craft standard
Analytical or Synthesis Essay
Thesis up front. Logical structure. Quotations framed, quoted, explained. Source claims introduced with signal phrases. A real counterargument, answered. In-text MLA citations and a Works Cited page. About 1,200 to 1,600 words.
Debate
Both cases at full strength, each grounded in a module text and a JStor source, then a reasoned judgment that earns its conclusion. Written, or recorded.
Letters or Diary
A consistent, convincing voice for each writer. Each entry tied to a real moment in a text. A clear arc across four to six entries. An analytical afterword of about 150 to 250 words that cites your sources.
Short Film or Screenplay
Deliberate framing, pacing, dialogue, and sound that carry the interpretation. A coherent short film, or a four to six page screenplay, with a short written note naming the texts and sources behind it.
Podcast or Oration
A clear spoken argument with a classical shape: introduction, body, conclusion. Delivery you control: pace, pauses, emphasis. One passage read aloud to effect. About 5 to 8 minutes, with a Works Cited page uploaded alongside.
