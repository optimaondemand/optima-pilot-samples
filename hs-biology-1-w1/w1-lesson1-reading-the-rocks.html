Stand at the edge of a deep canyon and you are reading a book. The bands of rock in the wall are its pages, the lowest band written first and the highest written last. Buried in those pages are the bones, shells, and imprints of creatures that lived and died across millions of years.

What a fossil is, and how one forms

A fossil is any preserved trace of past life: a bone, a shell, a leaf print, a footprint, a burrow. Most form the same patient way. An organism dies and is quickly buried in sediment (mud, sand, ash) before scavengers and decay can erase it. Over long stretches of time, layer after layer of sediment piles on top, pressing the lower layers into rock. Minerals seep into the buried remains and slowly replace them, turning bone to stone.

Far more often than not, nothing is preserved at all. Fossilization is the rare exception, which is why the fossil record is a library with many missing pages. But it is a real library nonetheless.

The word fossil comes from the Latin fossus, “dug up.” That is exactly what it is: something dug out of the ground that once was alive.

A layered history: stratigraphy

Because sediment settles from the top down, layers stack in time order. The study of these layers is called stratigraphy (from stratum, “a layer”). It rests on a simple, powerful idea called the law of superposition: in undisturbed rock, deeper layers are older and layers nearer the surface are younger. Picture a stack of newspapers tossed on the floor over a week. The one on the bottom is the oldest; the one on top is the newest. Rock layers work the same way.

Deeper layer = older · Shallower layer = younger

So the fossils in a layer are roughly as old as the layer itself, and the record reads, bottom to top, like a timeline.

Index fossils: clocks in the rock

Some species are especially useful for dating layers. An index fossil comes from a species that was geographically widespread (so it shows up in many places) but existed only for a short slice of time (so it pins a layer to a narrow age). Find that species in a rock layer anywhere in the world, and you know that layer is from the same time. Trilobites and certain ammonites are classic examples. An index fossil is a little like a postmark: it stamps a layer with a date.

Notice the logic: a fossil is a good clock precisely because the species changed. It appeared, spread, and then was replaced. A world of unchanging life would have no index fossils at all.

Transitional fossils: forms in between

The most striking fossils of all are transitional fossils: forms that carry a mix of features from an older group and a newer one, capturing a major change partway through.
