A pattern that needs explaining

Biogeography is the study of where living things are found across the globe. It turns up a pattern that cries out for explanation. If each kind of creature were simply placed wherever it best fit, we would expect the same climates around the world to hold the same animals. But they do not. The grasslands of Australia, Africa, and South America have similar climates yet wildly different mammals: pouched marsupials dominate Australia, while placental mammals dominate the others.

The pattern makes immediate sense, though, if species descend, with modification, from ancestors that lived nearby. Australia’s marsupials are concentrated there because they arose and diversified on a landmass long isolated from the others, with few placental mammals to compete with them. Geography, not climate alone, shaped who lives where.

Nearly all the world’s kangaroos, koalas, and other pouched mammals are found in Australia and almost nowhere else. The word biogeography joins bio-, “life,” geo-, “earth,” and -graphy, “writing.”

Islands tell the clearest story

Remote islands are biogeography’s sharpest test. Their species are often endemic (found there and nowhere else), yet they usually resemble, more than anything, the species of the nearest mainland. That is exactly the pattern expected if a few ancestors from the mainland reached the island long ago and then changed over generations in isolation. It is hard to explain if each island species were created independently and unrelated to its neighbors.

The Galápagos finches are the most famous example. On these volcanic islands far off the coast of South America live more than a dozen finch species found nowhere else, differing mainly in the size and shape of their beaks: some stout for crushing seeds, some slender for probing. Yet all of them resemble a single mainland finch from the nearby coast. The most natural reading: a few founders arrived from the mainland and, over time, their descendants diversified to use the different foods the islands offered.

A human story: two naturalists, one idea, and the courage to publish

The patterns of biogeography were among the clues that led two naturalists, working an ocean apart, to the same revolutionary idea.

As a young naturalist aboard the survey ship HMS Beagle, Charles Darwin spent years observing the natural world, including the Galápagos, and slowly assembled the idea of evolution by natural selection. Yet he delayed publishing for roughly twenty years, gathering more evidence and bracing for the storm he knew the idea would raise. Then, in 1858, a letter arrived from Alfred Russel Wallace, a naturalist working in the Malay Archipelago, who had independently arrived at the very same idea and mailed Darwin an essay describing it. Rather than race to claim sole credit, the two men’s work was presented jointly at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858, and Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859.

The episode shows courage, following evidence into long, public controversy, alongside the honesty of sharing credit for a discovery two people reached at once.
