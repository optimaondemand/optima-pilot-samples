The Field Journal

Read forWhat every living thing has in common at the molecular level, and why that is evidence of shared ancestry.

DNA carries information, and the genetic code turns that information into proteins. Now comes a remarkable fact: every living thing uses the same kind of molecule and very nearly the same code. A gene from a bacterium can be read by the machinery of a human cell.

One language for all of life

From bacteria to oak trees to whales, all known life stores its instructions in DNA (or, in some viruses, RNA) and reads those instructions with nearly the same genetic code: the same three-letter codons standing for the same amino acids. There is no biological reason a fish and a fern had to share a coding scheme. That they do is powerful evidence that all living things are branches of a single family tree, inheriting one ancient molecular language from a common ancestor.

This idea reaches straight back through everything you know about DNA: its structure, its base-pairing and copying, and the variation produced by mutation and meiosis. All of it is the raw material that molecular comparisons now read as a record of descent.

The more alike the sequence, the closer the kin

Here is the heart of molecular evidence. Over long stretches of time, small changes (mutations) accumulate in DNA and in the proteins it codes for. So the more similar two species’ DNA or protein sequences are, the more recently they shared a common ancestor; the more different, the longer ago their lineages split. Compare a protein such as cytochrome c (used in cellular respiration) or hemoglobin (which carries oxygen) across many species, and the degree of similarity lines up beautifully with how closely related those species appear from their anatomy and the fossil record.

More similar sequence → more recent common ancestor

Humans and chimpanzees, for example, have strikingly similar DNA, which tells us they share a relatively recent common ancestor. It does not mean humans evolved from chimpanzees: both species descend from that shared ancestral species, and each has been changing along its own branch ever since.

The molecules keep a record of descent, written in the order of their building blocks.

    When independent lines agree

    Molecular evidence is powerful on its own, but its real strength is this: it was gathered in a completely different way from fossils, from comparative anatomy, and from biogeography, and yet it points to the same family tree. When several independent lines of evidence, each able in principle to have disagreed, instead converge on one conclusion, the conclusion is hard to dismiss as coincidence. A whale’s flipper bones (anatomy), its walking ancestors (fossils), its pelvic remnants (anatomy again), and its DNA similarity to other mammals (molecular) all tell one story.

    This convergence is the foundation of strong scientific reasoning, and it is the reason scientists regard common descent as one of the best-supported ideas in all of biology.

    AI awareness · Machine learning and the tree of life

    AI awareness

    Comparing whole genomes by hand is impossible: the datasets are enormous. So scientists now use computational methods, including some machine-learning approaches, to compare millions of sequences and propose evolutionary trees far faster than any person could. That is the opportunity: handling genomic-scale data and suggesting relationships humans might never spot.

    Here is the pitfall: a computed tree is only as good as the data fed in and the assumptions built into the model, and these tools can be confidently wrong. A tree produced by an algorithm is a hypothesis to test against fossils, anatomy, and other data, not an authority to trust on its own. Its answer should be questioned exactly as any scientific claim is.

    Contemporary research

    Reading DNA from the deep past

    Recent research suggests that DNA can sometimes survive in fossils and ancient remains for tens of thousands of years. Scientists are currently recovering and reading ancient DNA, including genomes of our extinct relatives the Neanderthals and the Denisovans, which lets them study relationships directly from the molecules rather than only from bone shape. This work is still developing and demands great care to avoid contamination, but it is opening a new window: a way to read descent not just in living species, but in the ancient dead themselves.
