A Model Seed, on a Different Story

Mara works the night ferry and wants one thing: the seat in the harbor office that would let her stop working nights. A passenger leaves a sealed envelope and says to hand it to the harbor master only if she gets a fair price for it. She can sell it to a rival for a promotion, or deliver it and stay where she is. The cost she does not see: the rival is her brother.

Which want is concrete enough for a seed?

B

Your Story Seed

Across the four boxes, about 150 to 250 words. A seed is the smallest thing that can grow into a story: a character, a want, and a price they never saw coming.

Your character and their want A name and one line about who they are, then what they want badly. One or two sentences on the want. Make it a thing you could point to, not a feeling.
The wish or choice that seems to grant it A shortcut, a lie, a bargain, a new technology, or an enchanted object.
The cost they did not see coming Two or three sentences. What does getting the wish cost, and why could they not see it?
Conflict type and a possible ending Name the conflict at the center (person vs. person, person vs. self, person vs. nature, or person vs. society), then one sentence on how it might end. A guess is fine; you can change it.
  • A named character with one concrete want.
  • A wish or choice that grants the want, and a cost that follows from it.
  • Conflict type labeled and one possible ending included.
  • Proofread.

Your Dilemma

Across the five boxes, about 250 to 300 words. Both costs should hurt about equally.

Your protagonist Who they are and the one thing they want most. Two or three sentences.
The impossible choice, in one sentence Make it two-sided: Option A or Option B. A colon can reveal it: She had two doors: ...
What Option A costs One short paragraph with at least one conditional: If she chooses A, then ... and she will lose ...
What Option B costs One short paragraph with at least one conditional. It must hurt as much as the first.
Why is this choice hard for this character in particular? Two or three sentences. Name the two forces inside them that make both options live.
Use at least three of these Word Study terms accurately in your dilemma
  • ambiguity
  • semi-barbaric
  • impartial
  • fervent
  • deliberation
  • A protagonist a reader can picture, and the one thing they want most.
  • One clear two-sided choice with a real cost named for each side, in conditional sentences.
  • An explanation of why this character, specifically, finds both options live.
  • Three Word Study terms used well; proofread.

An Opening That Plants

“The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could; but when he ventured upon insult, I vowed revenge.”Poe, “The Cask of Amontillado,” the first sentence

One sentence gives a want, a past, and a promise that something will cost. A planted detail can be an object, a habit, a line of dialogue, or a setting detail. Each reads as ordinary the first time and as a clue the second.

Detail planted: Mara checks the ferry lamp three times each night. What it anticipates: she checks what she cannot trust. Where it pays off: she checks the envelope seal three times before she decides.

Which sentence is a planted detail?

B

Your Opening

Your opening, 150 to 300 words Introduce the character and want from your seed and lean toward your dilemma without announcing it. Show the setting and mood with what the senses report. Leave out verdict words like terrible or wonderful. Plant three details inside it.
