Reel Choices

Choose a movie where one of the characters has to make an important decision, then examine that decision the way an economist would.

Focus question: How do scarcity, trade-offs, and opportunity cost shape a character's hardest decision?

1

Set the scene. Name the movie and the character, then describe the decision, what was at stake, and the moment or scene where it happens. Explain the pressures on the character.

A strong answer names the film, the character, and the exact scene, then says what the character stands to win or lose. Pressures are specific: a deadline, a person depending on them, limited money, or danger. A character who must pick between two jobs with the rent due shows a clear pressure.
2

Weigh the options. Describe each option the character had. For each one, explain the scarcity behind the choice, the trade-offs, the opportunity cost, and the short-term and long-term outcomes, using specific events from the film.

For each option, say what it would gain and what it would use up, then separate what happens soon from what happens later. Name the scarce resource forcing the choice: time, money, safety, or trust. A character who can reach only one of two places before a deadline is limited by time.
3

Name the cost. State what the character gave up by choosing as they did. Be concrete about what the next-best option would have delivered.

Opportunity cost is the next-best option the character gave up, not every option on the table. Say exactly what that option would have delivered, in terms the film shows. A shop owner who takes the bigger lease over a second-best partnership gives up the partnership's steady income.
4

Make your own call. Say which choice you would have made, and justify it with specific film details and economic reasoning.

Choose one option and defend it with at least two details from the film and the lesson's terms: scarcity, trade-off, opportunity cost. Compare what you would gain with what the next-best option would cost you. A reason that cites no scene is a feeling, not an argument.

Reading and Analyzing the Production Possibilities Curve

Lesson 1.02 introduced the Production Possibilities Curve (PPC) as one line of vocabulary. This assignment asks you to actually read one — to look at a real PPC diagram and use it to reason about efficiency, scarcity, and opportunity cost the way an economist would, rather than just defining the term.

Focus question: What does the shape and position of a production possibilities curve reveal about scarcity, efficiency, and opportunity cost?

1

Classify the points. Look at points A, B, C, and D on the diagram above. For each point, state whether it represents efficient, inefficient, or unattainable production, and explain how you can tell from where it sits relative to the curve.

Position is the whole test. For each letter, say where the point sits (on the curve, inside it, or beyond it) and what that says about the resources in use. On a bread-and-bicycles curve, a point beyond the curve asks for more than the bakeries and workshops can make together.
2

Compare A and B. Compare points A and B. If the economy moved from point A to point B, what would it be giving up, and what would it be gaining? Write this as a specific opportunity cost statement (e.g., "moving from A to B costs ___ in order to gain ___").

Read both coordinates off the axes and subtract. State the amount of each good, with its name. On a bread-and-bicycles curve, moving from 5 to 10 bread costs 6 bicycles.
3

Explain point C. Point C sits inside the curve. Name one real reason an economy might actually be producing at a point like C instead of on the curve (think about unemployment, unused factories, or underused resources).

Name a specific cause and say what is idle or misused. A farm with good land and no one trained to run its new harvesters has resources that exist but are not producing.
4

Shift the curve. Point D sits outside the curve — currently unattainable. Name two things that could cause the entire curve to shift outward, making a point like D possible in the future.

A shift changes what the economy is able to produce, which is different from choosing another point. Name two separate causes and say what each adds. For each cause, say whether it adds resources or lets the same resources produce more, and why that moves the whole curve rather than one point.
5

One diagram, three ideas. In 3–5 sentences, explain how this one diagram manages to show scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost all at once.

Take the three ideas in turn and point to a specific feature of the diagram for each, using the point letters. A definition with no feature of the graph attached does not answer the prompt.

Chapter 1

Scarcity Is Permanent, Not Temporary

Scarcity exists because resources — money, time, raw materials, labor, land, knowledge — are always limited, while human wants keep expanding. Farmland is finite even with modern machinery; oil, coal, and natural gas cannot be replaced at the rate they are extracted; every worker has only twenty-four hours in a day. This is different from a shortage, which is a temporary lack of one product. Scarcity is the permanent backdrop: no amount of innovation lets every want be met at once, which is why it is considered the starting point of all economics.

Chapter 2

Choice: Deciding How to Use What You Have

A choice is how limited resources get allocated among competing wants. A student choosing between studying and going out, a business deciding whether to expand or invest in equipment, a government weighing education against defense spending — all of these are the same underlying act: setting priorities because time and money cannot cover every possibility. Every choice involves sacrifice; to pursue one goal is to set the other aside.

Two tools economists use to evaluate a choice: cost-benefit analysis weighs what is gained against what is lost, and utility is the satisfaction a person gets from a good, service, or choice. They explain why two people facing the identical trade-off — a concert ticket versus saving for a phone — can rationally choose differently: they place different utility on each option.

Chapter 3

Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up when a choice is made. It is bigger than the money spent: if you spend $20 on a book, the opportunity cost is not the $20 itself but the movie night or savings contribution you gave up instead. Opportunity cost applies at every scale. A worker choosing extra shifts over family time gains income at the cost of relationships. A business that commits funds to new technology may have to give up a new product line. A government that funds a highway system may have fewer dollars for schools or hospitals. In every case, the true cost of a decision is not just what is spent, but what is forgone.

Time deserves special attention here, because unlike money it can never be replaced once spent. An hour of video games might be relaxing, but its opportunity cost is the studying, exercise, or rest that hour could have held instead. Recognizing this is what separates careless decision-making from thoughtful decision-making: nothing is truly free, and naming the cost you do not see is as important as naming the choice you do.
