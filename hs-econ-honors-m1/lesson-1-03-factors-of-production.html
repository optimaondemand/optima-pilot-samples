Factor Factory

Every product or service you've ever used exists because someone combined land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship to bring it into being. This assignment asks you to invent a small business and trace exactly how each factor of production makes it run.

Focus question: How do land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship combine to create a real business?

1

Invent your business. Describe a small business you're starting: what it sells and who its customers are.

Say what the business sells and who buys it in a sentence or two. A specific business works better than a general one: a bike repair stand outside a school, not "a store." Pick something whose inputs you can picture.
2

Identify your land. Name the natural resources or physical space your business needs, and explain why each one matters to your product.

Land is the natural resources and the physical space, not the money spent on them. For a bike repair stand: the patch of ground it sits on and the water used to rinse mud off. Say why each one matters to the product.
3

Identify your labor. Describe the workers your business needs and the skills they bring.

List the jobs the business needs done, then name the skill each worker brings. For the repair stand: a mechanic who can straighten a bent wheel, and someone who handles customers and schedules. A skill, not just a job title.
4

Identify your capital. Describe the tools, equipment, or buildings your business needs to operate, and how each one adds value.

Capital is human-made and used in production: tools, equipment, buildings. Name each item and say how it adds value. A stand and a wrench set let one mechanic fix more bikes in the same time. Cash in the till is not on this list.
5

Explain your entrepreneurship. Describe the risk you're taking as the owner, and how you're combining land, labor, and capital in a way no one else has.

Entrepreneurship is the owner's decisions and risk. State what you stand to lose if the business fails, then show how you combine your land, labor, and capital in a way a competitor has not. For the repair stand: buying tools before earning anything, and offering repairs at the school gate.

Land and Labor

Chapter 1

Land: The Gift of Nature

Land covers not just ground to build on, but every natural resource that comes from the Earth: fertile soil, forests, mineral deposits, fossil fuels, sunlight, wind, and water. Land is a "gift of nature" — it exists independent of human effort — but it is finite and unevenly distributed. Some regions have plentiful farmland and water; others have deserts or scarce rainfall. That unevenness shapes trade, energy policy, and national wealth, which is why decisions about how to allocate land (farming, industry, conservation) carry real long-term trade-offs like soil depletion or deforestation if mismanaged.

Chapter 2

Labor: Human Effort, Physical and Mental

Labor is the human effort applied to production — from a construction crew to a software developer. Like land, labor is scarce: limited hours in a day, and workers vary in skill and experience. That scarcity affects wages directly (specialized skills in short supply command higher pay) and forces businesses and governments to make trade-offs about where to deploy workers. Labor is also the one factor you can grow deliberately: investing in human capital through education and training raises what the same number of workers can produce, though that investment itself has an opportunity cost.

Part 2

Capital

Chapter 3Capital: What Humans Build to Aid Production
Chapter 3

Capital: What Humans Build to Aid Production

Capital is the human-made resources used in production — machinery, tools, factories, roads, computers. Unlike land, nothing about capital is naturally occurring; it exists because someone built it to make labor and land more productive. Physical capital (machines, buildings) and human capital (skills, expertise) both let an economy produce more with the same raw inputs. Capital is not unlimited either: it wears out, becomes obsolete, and competes for the same investment dollars as other priorities, so allocating it always involves opportunity cost.

Part 3

Entrepreneurship

Chapter 4Entrepreneurship: The Spark That Organizes the Other Three
Chapter 4

Entrepreneurship: The Spark That Organizes the Other Three

Entrepreneurship is the drive to innovate, take calculated risks, and combine land, labor, and capital into a working enterprise. Entrepreneurs spot opportunities, decide how to allocate scarce resources, and absorb the uncertainty of outcomes that are never guaranteed. Without entrepreneurial activity, resources can sit underutilized and innovation stalls — entrepreneurship is what actually puts the other three factors to work.

Put it together: bread needs farmland and wheat (land), farmers and bakers (labor), ovens and delivery trucks (capital), and a bakery owner who organizes the whole process (entrepreneurship). Nations with more of each factor — and stronger entrepreneurial cultures — tend to produce more and enjoy higher standards of living.
