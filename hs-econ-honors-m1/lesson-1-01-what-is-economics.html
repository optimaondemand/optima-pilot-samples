Today's Focus

How do people make decisions when they cannot have everything they want?

You already think like an economist more than you realize. Every time you choose one thing and give up another — a lunch, an evening, a purchase — you are wrestling with the problem this whole course is built on: resources are limited, wants are not. Economics is the discipline that studies how people, businesses, and governments handle that gap.

Decision Dissection

Every decision you make is really an economics problem in disguise: something given up so something else can be gained. This assignment asks you to take one real decision from your own life and examine it the way an economist would.

Focus question: What can a real decision you made teach you about scarcity, trade-offs, and opportunity cost?

1

Choose a real decision. Pick one actual decision you made recently, something with a real trade-off (how to spend a free evening, a purchase, a choice between two commitments). Describe the decision and what was at stake for you.

A decision with a real trade-off has two or more options you actually wanted and could not have together. An evening you spent studying instead of going to a friend's game qualifies. A choice you never weighed does not.
2

Describe your alternatives. Explain at least two options you actually considered and what choosing each one would have meant for you.

Describe each option in terms of what it would have given you and what it would have used up: money, time, energy, or another commitment.
3

Name the opportunity cost. For the option you chose, identify specifically what you gave up (time, money, another experience) by not choosing the next-best alternative. Be concrete, not general.

Opportunity cost is only the next-best option you gave up, not every option you skipped. If you took the extra work shift and your second choice was the game, the game is the cost. Name it precisely: the hours, the dollars, the experience.
4

Reflect and justify. Looking back, do you think you made the right call? Use the language of scarcity, trade-offs, and opportunity cost to explain your reasoning, not just your gut feeling.

Judge the choice by comparing what you gained with the opportunity cost. Use the terms scarcity, trade-off, and opportunity cost by name, and say what limited resource forced the choice.

Subject vocabulary
Incentive/in-SEN-tiv/A reward or reason that pushes people toward one choice over another.
Macroeconomics/MAK-roh-ek-uh-NOM-iks/The study of the whole economy, including unemployment, inflation, and growth.
Microeconomics/my-kroh-ek-uh-NOM-iks/The study of the choices made by individual people, households, and businesses.
Opportunity Cost/op-er-TOO-nih-tee kawst/The value of the next-best choice you give up when you decide.
Recession/rih-SESH-un/A period when the economy shrinks. It is usually measured as two quarters in a row of falling output.
Scarcity/SKAIR-sih-tee/The condition of having limited resources while human wants have no limit.
Trade-Off/TRAYD-awf/Giving up one thing in order to get another.

Chapter 1

Scarcity: the Problem Every Society Must Solve

Economics is sometimes called the science of scarcity. Scarcity does not mean nothing is available — it means there is never enough of everything to satisfy every possible use for it. Farmland used to grow food cannot also grow biofuel; a forest logged for timber cannot also stay untouched wilderness. Because resources — tangible ones like land, money, and machinery, and intangible ones like time and skill — are limited, every decision involves a trade-off: choosing one option means giving up another.

Because scarcity is universal, every society, no matter its politics or geography, has to answer three questions: What should be produced? How should it be produced? For whom should it be produced? A market economy lets consumer demand and business competition answer those questions; a centrally planned economy has government officials answer them. Either way, the answers shape who has access to what, and how wealth moves through a society.

Chapter 2

Two Ways to Zoom: Micro and Macro

Economics splits into two branches. Microeconomics zooms in on individual decision-makers — a household deciding what to buy, a worker deciding where to apply, a business deciding what to produce — and asks why ticket prices spike in peak season or how a sale changes buying habits. Macroeconomics zooms out to the whole economy — unemployment, inflation, growth, trade — and asks why an entire economy slides into a recession or grows quickly. You will use both lenses all semester: micro to explain one firm's decision, macro to explain the system those decisions add up to.

Worth noticing: economists lean on two more ideas constantly. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative you gave up to make your choice. Incentives are the financial, social, or personal motivations that push people toward one choice over another. You will meet both terms again and again this semester — they are not one-lesson vocabulary, they are how economists think.
