Chapter 1

About This Source

This is a diplomat’s report. On August 4, 1914, Sir Edward Goschen, the British ambassador in Berlin, carried out his last duties before Britain and Germany went to war. Afterward he wrote an account of what was said and sent it to his foreign minister, Sir Edward Grey. The British government published it on August 27, 1914, so it was written for the British public as well as for Grey.

Three German officials appear. Gottlieb von Jagow was the foreign minister. Theobald von Bethmann-Hollweg was the chancellor, the head of the German government. The “scrap of paper” is the 1839 treaty guaranteeing Belgium’s neutrality.

Read this as a historian should. Ask who wrote it, who it was for, and what the writer wanted his readers to believe. A diplomat reports what he heard, but he also chooses what to put down. This is a firsthand account, but it comes from one side of the conversation.

Chapter 2

Jagow, in Plain English

Plain version, written for this page. These are not Goschen's words.

Goschen called on the Secretary of State, Herr von Jagow, that afternoon. As his instructions told him, he asked, in the name of the British government, whether the German government would refrain from violating Belgian neutrality. Jagow replied at once that he was sorry, but his answer must be “No.” German troops had crossed the frontier that morning, so Belgian neutrality had already been violated.

Jagow went over the reasons again. Germany had to advance into France by the quickest and easiest way, to get well ahead with its operations and strike a decisive blow as early as possible. He called it a matter of life and death. By the more southern route, with its few roads and its strong fortresses, Germany could not have hoped to get through without formidable opposition and great loss of time. Lost time would have meant time gained by the Russians to bring up their troops to the German frontier.

Chapter 3

Jagow, in the Original

The original: dispatch dated London, August 8, 1914

In accordance with the instructions contained in your telegram of the 4th inst., I called upon the Secretary of State that afternoon and inquired, in the name of his Majesty’s Government, whether the Imperial Government would refrain from violating Belgian neutrality. Herr von Jagow at once replied that he was sorry to say that his answer must be “No,” as, in consequence of the German troops having crossed the frontier that morning, Belgian neutrality had been already violated.

Herr von Jagow again went into the reasons why the Imperial Government had been obliged to take this step, namely, that they had to advance into France by the quickest and easiest way, so as to be able to get well ahead with their operations and endeavour to strike some decisive blow as early as possible.

It was a matter of life and death for them, as if they had gone by the more southern route they could not have hoped, in view of the paucity of roads and the strength of the fortresses, to have got through without formidable opposition entailing great loss of time. This loss of time would have meant time gained by the Russians for bringing up their troops to the German frontier.

Chapter 4

The Chancellor, in Plain English

Plain version, written for this page. These are not Goschen's words.

Goschen then said he would like to see the Chancellor, since it might be the last chance he had. The chancellor begged him to come. Goschen found him very agitated. The chancellor spoke for about twenty minutes. He said that Britain's step was terrible: just for a word, “neutrality,” which in wartime had so often been disregarded, just for a “scrap of paper,” Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation that wanted nothing better than to be friends with her. All his efforts toward that friendship had been made useless, and the policy to which he had devoted himself since he took office had “tumbled down like a house of cards.” What Britain had done was unthinkable, he said. It was like striking a man from behind while he was fighting for his life against two assailants. He held Great Britain responsible for all the terrible events that might happen.

Goschen protested strongly. Jagow and the chancellor wished him to understand that, for strategic reasons, it was a matter of “life and death” for Germany to advance through Belgium. In the same way, Goschen said, he wished the chancellor to understand that it was a matter of “life and death” for the honour of Great Britain to keep her solemn engagement to defend Belgium's neutrality if it was attacked. That solemn compact had to be kept, or no one could have confidence in engagements given by Great Britain in the future. The chancellor said, “But at what price will that compact have been kept? Has the British Government thought of that?” Goschen hinted as plainly as he could that fear of consequences could hardly be an excuse for breaking solemn engagements. But the chancellor was so excited, so overcome by the news, and so little disposed to hear reason, that Goschen did not argue further.

Chapter 5

The Chancellor, in the Original

The original, continued. [...] marks a cut.

[...]

I then said that I should like to go and see the Chancellor, as it might be, perhaps, the last time I should have an opportunity of seeing him. He begged me to do so. I found the Chancellor very agitated. His Excellency at once began a harangue, which lasted for about twenty minutes. He said that the step taken by his Majesty’s Government was terrible to a degree; just for a word--“neutrality,” a word which in war time had so often been disregarded--just for a scrap of paper, Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation who desired nothing better than to be friends with her. All his efforts in that direction had been rendered useless by this last terrible step, and the policy to which, as I knew, he had devoted himself since his accession to office had tumbled down like a house of cards. What we had done was unthinkable; it was like striking a man from behind while he was fighting for his life against two assailants. He held Great Britain responsible for all the terrible events that might happen.

I protested strongly against that statement, and said that, in the same way as he and Herr von Jagow wished me to understand that for strategical reasons it was a matter of life and death to Germany to advance through Belgium and violate the latter’s neutrality, so I would wish him to understand that it was, so to speak, a matter of “life and death” for the honour of Great Britain that she should keep her solemn engagement to do her utmost to defend Belgium’s neutrality if attacked. That solemn compact simply had to be kept, or what confidence could anyone have in engagements given by Great Britain in the future? The Chancellor said, “But at what price will that compact have been kept? Has the British Government thought of that?” I hinted to his Excellency as plainly as I could that fear of consequences could hardly be regarded as an excuse for breaking solemn engagements, but his Excellency was so excited, so evidently overcome by the news of our action, and so little disposed to hear reason, that I refrained from adding fuel to the flame by further argument.

A Note for You

From the page

Notice how the German foreign minister explains the invasion of Belgium as a matter of military need, while the chancellor and the ambassador argue in terms of promises and honor. Hold on to that contrast.
