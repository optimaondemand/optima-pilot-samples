English 3 · Module 3 · Independent Reading
20 pointsOne recording, about 3 minutesTwo attemptsDue at the end of Week 8
Three minutes on the book you chose
 
At the start of the quarter you chose a book from the Optima Independent Reading library. This is the one check on that reading: a recording of about three minutes, in whichever of the four shapes below fits you and the book. Jane Eyre is this quarter’s study text and is not eligible; any other book from the library, or one your teacher approved, is.
Pick one of four
 
1Oral Book Responseabout 3 minutes
Answer these five out loud, in order. Notes in front of you are fine; a script read word for word is not.
1What is the book, and how far did you get?
2Pick one character. What do they want, and what is in the way?
3Describe a moment where that character makes a choice you would not have made. Why did they make it?
4Read one sentence or short passage you marked, and say why that one.
5Who should read this book, and who should skip it?
2Dramatic Readingabout 3 minutes
1Choose a passage of roughly 150 to 300 words where a character is under pressure: deciding, arguing, afraid, or hiding something.
2Read it aloud as that character. Decide before you record what they want in this moment, then let your pace, volume and pauses show it.
3Then talk for about a minute: who is this character, what do they want here, and what did you do with your voice to show it? Name one specific choice you made.
3Make the Caseabout 3 minutes
You are a guest on a books podcast and the host has given you three minutes to make one argument. House rule of the show: you have to take the side most listeners will not.
1Name what you are arguing about, and say where it falls in the book. Pick something people genuinely disagree over: a moral dilemma, a turn nobody saw coming, or a decision a character made that readers still argue about.
2Take the unpopular side and say it in your first sentence. If most readers think the character was right, argue that they were wrong. If nobody could disagree with your claim, it is not an argument yet.
3Give two pieces of evidence from the text: a line, a scene, something a character says or does. Say roughly where each one falls.
4Answer the strongest objection, the one the host would come back at you with. State it fairly first, then answer it.
5Land it. Finish with one sentence a listener would still be turning over after the episode ends.
4A Different Endingabout 3 minutes
Only take this one if you finished the book. You are going to replace the ending, and you cannot rewrite an ending you never got to.
1Say how the book actually ends, in two or three sentences. Be accurate. The rest of this only works if your listener knows what you are changing.
2Name the exact point where your version branches off, then describe your ending in detail. What happens, who it happens to, and how does it finish?
3Explain why yours is better for this book. Not happier, and not kinder to your favourite character, but truer to what the story has been about. Name the idea or theme you are serving.
4Say what your ending costs. Every ending gives something up. Name what is lost by not keeping the one the author wrote.
What earns full credit
 
›You clearly read the book.
›You point at something specific, a moment or a line, rather than the story in general.
›You say something that is yours: a judgement, a disagreement, a question.
›You can be heard clearly the whole way through.
What a summary cannot do for you
 
All four options are built so that a plot summary will not get you through them. Here is what your teacher is actually listening for.
›Name where you are. Give a page, a chapter, or “about two thirds through.” Summaries do not carry page numbers. Readers do.
›Read or quote something exact. One short line, said out loud. It is the fastest way there is to show you held the book.
›Say what you thought was wrong. Or dull, or surprising. A summary has no opinions. You do, and that is the part being assessed.
›Stay on one moment. Retelling the whole plot is the clearest sign of a book someone did not read. One scene closely beats the whole story vaguely.
›Be honest about how far you got. “I stopped at chapter nine because ___” earns more than a confident account of an ending you never reached.
A note on AI tools
 
A summary tool can tell you what happens in a book. It cannot tell you which page you stopped on, which line you read twice, or what you thought was wrong. Those are the things these options ask for.
