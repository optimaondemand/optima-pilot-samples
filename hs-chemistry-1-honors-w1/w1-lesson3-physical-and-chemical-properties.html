Properties: how we tell substances apart

Melted ice is still water; rusted iron is no longer iron. That single difference — whether the identity of the substance survived — is one of the sharpest tools in chemistry. Today you'll learn to describe matter by its properties, to tell physical changes from chemical ones, and to sort properties into the two kinds that behave differently when you change the amount.

A property is any characteristic that helps you tell one substance from another. A physical property can be observed without changing what the substance is — color, hardness, density, melting point, boiling point, electrical conductivity. Some you can see at a glance (color); others show up only during a physical change, a change in form or state that leaves the substance's identity intact. Melting wax, dissolving sugar in coffee, and steam condensing on a lid are all physical changes: rearranged, restated, but chemically the same stuff.

A chemical property describes how a substance changes into a different substance — or refuses to. Flammability, the tendency to rust, acidity, and toxicity are chemical properties. You can only observe a chemical property by letting (or trying to let) a chemical change happen, which always produces one or more new substances with new properties. Iron rusting, a match burning, food rotting, copper dissolving in acid — in each, what you end with is genuinely different matter from what you started with.

The one question that decides it

Ask: “After the change, is it still the same substance?” If yes → physical. If a new substance with new properties has formed → chemical. Clues that point to chemical change: an unexpected color shift, gas bubbling out, a solid forming from liquids, light or heat released, or a smell that wasn't there before.

Physical change — identity survives

Ice → liquid water → steam · sugar dissolving · chopping wood · bending copper. Often reversible. Still the same substance.

Chemical change — new substance forms

Iron rusting · wood burning · bread baking · a banana browning. New properties appear. Usually not easily reversed.

A look back · When chemistry learned to weigh

In the 1770s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier insisted on doing something earlier investigators had mostly skipped: weighing everything, before and after, including the gases. When he heated metals in sealed vessels, the careful numbers told a story the eye could not — the metal gained exactly the mass that the air inside lost. Burning was not a substance escaping (the old “phlogiston” idea); it was the metal combining with a gas he named oxygen. By trusting the balance over the assumption, Lavoisier turned chemistry into a quantitative science — one where a chemical change is something you can measure, not just watch.

Extensive vs. intensive properties

Properties also split a second way — by whether they depend on how much matter you have. An extensive property depends on the amount: mass and volume are extensive, because a gallon of milk has more of both than a cup of milk. An intensive property does not depend on the amount: temperature, density, color, and boiling point stay the same no matter how big the sample is. Pour the gallon and the cup together (both at 20 °C) and the temperature is still 20 °C — it didn't add up.

This matters more than it first looks. Because intensive properties don't change with size, they are how we identify substances. Density is the star example: every pure sample of gold has the same density regardless of whether it's a coin or a bar.

Properties you can't ignore: reading a hazard diamond

Because chemical properties like flammability and reactivity can be dangerous, substances are labeled with a four-color hazard diamond (the NFPA 704 system). Each colored diamond rates one kind of hazard from 0 (none) to 4 (severe). Knowing how to read it is a real-world application of everything in this lesson — it is properties, turned into safety information at a glance.

Red = flammability · Blue = health hazard · Yellow = reactivity · White = special hazards (oxidizer, reacts with water, corrosive, …)

Safety

A high number on the red diamond means highly flammable; a 4 on the blue diamond means a brief exposure could be fatal. In any lab — physical or virtual — read the hazard diamond before you handle a substance, and follow the safety practices.
