The states of matter

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass, and on Earth we usually meet it in three familiar states. But rather than take the three states on trust, let’s do what a chemist does: observe the particles first, then name what we see.

    Now put names to what you saw. The arrangement where particles are locked in place, holding a definite shape and volume, is a solid. The one where particles stay close but slide past one another — keeping their volume but flowing to the container’s shape — is a liquid. The one where particles fly apart to fill all available space, with no fixed shape or volume, is a gas. Your squeeze test should have shown the gas particles moving closer, not shrinking — the amount of matter never changed. (A fourth state, plasma — a hot, electrically charged gas — makes up the interiors of stars, lightning, and some screens, but solids, liquids, and gases are what you’ll handle most.)

    Mass, weight, and a law that holds

    The mass of an object measures the amount of matter in it. Weight is something different — it is the force of gravity pulling on that mass. The distinction is easy to feel with a thought experiment: an astronaut who travels to the Moon has the exact same mass there as on Earth, but only about one-sixth the weight, because the Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's. Her amount of matter never changed; the pull on it did.

    Helpful to know

    A balance compares an unknown mass against known masses, so it reads the same on the Moon as on Earth. A spring scale measures force, so it reads weight — and would give a smaller number on the Moon. When chemists say “mass,” they mean the amount of matter, full stop.

    One of the most important discoveries in the whole history of chemistry is the law of conservation of matter: in any ordinary change — melting, dissolving, burning, reacting — the total quantity of matter does not change. Matter is rearranged, never destroyed. When sugar ferments into the alcohol and carbon dioxide in beer, the total mass before equals the total mass after. When a car battery discharges, the lead, lead oxide, and acid turn into lead sulfate and water with no loss of matter at all — it is just bookkeeping at the atomic level.

    Then & Now · How we learned this

    For a long time, burning looked like matter being destroyed — a log becomes a little ash. Careful weighing eventually showed the missing mass had escaped as invisible gases; nothing was actually lost. That insight, won by measuring instead of assuming, is why every chemical equation you balance this year must have the same number of atoms on both sides. The law isn't a rule we impose — it's what the evidence demands.

    Sorting all of matter

    Any sample of matter in the universe can be sorted with a single set of questions. First: is the composition fixed everywhere, or can it vary? That splits matter into pure substances (fixed composition — every sample is identical, like table sugar, which is always 42.1% carbon, 6.5% hydrogen, and 51.4% oxygen by mass) and mixtures (composition can vary, and the parts can be separated by physical means).

    Pure substances split again. An element cannot be broken into anything simpler by chemical means — iron, oxygen, gold, copper. A compound is two or more elements chemically joined, and it can be broken down: heating mercury(II) oxide releases liquid mercury and oxygen gas. Mixtures split too: a homogeneous mixture (also called a solution) looks uniform throughout — air, salt water, a sports drink — while a heterogeneous mixture has visibly different regions — oil-and-vinegar dressing, granite, a chocolate-chip cookie.

    Common mistake

    “Pure” in chemistry does not mean “healthy” or “clean” — it means one kind of substance, fixed composition. Pure water and pure table salt are both pure substances; so is pure carbon monoxide, which is deadly. And a clear, colorless solution like salt water is still a mixture, even though it looks like a single thing.

    Where the cutting stops: atoms & molecules

    Return to the opening question. Keep halving a piece of gold and eventually you reach a single atom — the smallest particle of an element that still is that element. Divide it further and it is no longer gold. The word comes from the Greek atomos, “uncuttable.” The idea is ancient (the Greek thinkers Leucippus and Democritus proposed it in the fifth century BCE), but it stayed a philosophical guess until the early 1800s, when the British teacher John Dalton backed it with careful measurements.

    Atoms are almost unimaginably small. It would take roughly 7,000 carbon atoms lined up to span the width of a single strand of spider silk. Often atoms join into molecules — two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds. Some elements travel as molecules of identical atoms (oxygen as O₂); compounds are molecules of different atoms (water as H₂O, with two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen).

    Worth knowing · Seeing the unseeable

    For most of history, atoms could only be imagined. Then instruments like the scanning tunneling microscope let scientists map individual atoms on a surface — each bump in the image is one atom. Today, AI tools help clean up and interpret images like these, spotting patterns faster than a person could. That's powerful — but an AI-enhanced image is still a model that must be checked against the raw measurements. AI can help you see; it cannot decide for you what is really there. That judgment stays yours.

    Three Distinctions People Mix Up

    Compound vs. homogeneous mixture

    ✓ Compound: pure water, H₂O — hydrogen and oxygen chemically bonded in a fixed 2:1 ratio, separable only by a chemical change.

    ✗ Not a compound: salt water — it looks just as uniform, but the salt and water are only physically mingled in a ratio you can change, and boiling separates them with no chemical change. Looking uniform is not the same as being one substance.

    Element vs. atom

    ✓ Correct: a gold bar is the element gold (a kind of matter, made of one kind of atom); a single gold atom is one individual particle of it.

    ✗ Mistake: calling a whole gold nugget “an atom.” The element is the kind; the atom is the smallest single particle of that kind.

    Physical mixture vs. chemical bonding

    ✓ Chemical bonding: heat iron and sulfur together and they react into iron sulfide — a new substance with new properties, no longer magnetic.

    ✗ Just a physical mixture: merely stirring iron filings and sulfur powder — still iron and still sulfur, and a magnet pulls the iron right back out. Mixing is not bonding.

    Then → Now → Future · Learning to sort matter

    Then: early chemists and alchemists could only sort substances by how they looked and behaved — melting, dissolving, burning — slowly separating true elements from compounds and mixtures by patient trial. Now: materials scientists do the reverse — they design new compounds and alloys on purpose, arranging elements to get a property they want, from lightweight aircraft alloys to the compounds inside phone batteries. Future: a major open goal is to predict a brand-new material’s properties from its composition before ever making it — sorting the possibilities by computer so the right substance can be built the first time.

    The big idea to carry forward

    Matter comes in states, it is never created or destroyed in ordinary changes, and every sample can be sorted by two questions: fixed composition or not, and one component or more. At the bottom of it all sit atoms — where, at last, the cutting stops.
