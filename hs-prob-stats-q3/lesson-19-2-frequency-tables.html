From a pile of answers to a table

Suppose you ask 25 students, “How do you usually get to school?” and write the answers in the order they arrive:

Car, Bus, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike, Car, Bus, Bus, Walk, Car, Car, Bike, Bus, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bus, Car, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike

That is raw data: honest, complete, and nearly unreadable. The first move, the move John Graunt made with the Bills of Mortality, is to sort and count. The number of times a value occurs is its frequency. A frequency table lists each value or category beside its frequency. (The data here is a constructed example.)

How to tally without losing anyone

Counting by eye through twenty-five words invites a skipped or double-counted answer. Use the discipline of a careful clerk:

  1. List the categories once, down the left side.
  2. Go through the raw list one entry at a time, in order, and make one tally mark by the matching category as you cross the entry out.
  3. Count the marks. Then check: the frequencies must add up to n, the number of individuals. If they do not, someone was lost or counted twice.
Way to schoolFrequency
Car10
Bus8
Walk4
Bike3
Total25

Ten plus eight plus four plus three is 25: every student accounted for. That total check is not decoration; it is the habit that catches errors before they become conclusions.

Relative frequency: answering “what fraction?”

“Ten students take the car” is a fact about this class. “Two-fifths of students take the car” is a statement we can compare with other classes. The relative frequency of a value is its frequency divided by the total:

Formularelative frequency = frequency ÷ n

Multiply by 100 to express it as a percent. Worked example. For the car riders, 10/25 = 0.40 = 40%. Do each category:

Way to schoolFrequencyRelative frequencyPercent
Car1010/25 = 0.4040%
Bus88/25 = 0.3232%
Walk44/25 = 0.1616%
Bike33/25 = 0.1212%
Total251.00100%

The second check: relative frequencies add to 1 (percents to 100%). Here 0.40 + 0.32 + 0.16 + 0.12 = 1.00. Why must they? Because together the categories contain every individual: each individual is counted once, so the pieces of the whole add to the whole.

When Rounding Spoils the Total

Time≈ 6 min
Status—

Not every division comes out clean. Suppose 30 students choose among four clubs with frequencies 5, 6, 8, and 11. Change how many decimal places you keep and watch the total.

ClubFrequencyExact fractionPercent

Nothing is wrong. Each value was rounded, and rounding errors can pile up a tenth of a percent. The honest practice has two parts: round only at the end (keep exact fractions while you work), and if a published table totals 100.1% or 99.9%, say so in a note (“percents may not sum to 100 because of rounding”) instead of quietly nudging one entry. A total that is off by a visible mistake, on the other hand, such as 108%, means an error you must find.

Frequency tables work for numerical variables too, when the values repeat. Twenty students report how many books they read last month (constructed example). Each value is a row:

Books readFrequencyRelative frequencyCumulative relative frequency
020.100.10
150.250.35
270.350.70
340.200.90
420.101.00
Total201.00

The cumulative relative frequency is a running total of the relative frequencies from the top: 0.10, then 0.10 + 0.25 = 0.35, then 0.35 + 0.35 = 0.70, and so on, ending at 1.00. Its job is to answer “at most” questions. What fraction of these students read at most 2 books? Read the row for 2: 0.70, or 70%. (Check by counting: 2 + 5 + 7 = 14 students, and 14/20 = 0.70.) Cumulative columns only make sense when the values have a natural order, so they belong to numerical data, not to categories like “car” and “bus.”

Why Relative Frequency Is the Honest Way to Compare

Time≈ 6 min
Status—

Section A has 20 students and 8 say they prefer taking a course online. Section B has 50 students and 15 say the same. Which section is more in favor? By raw counts, Section B (15 beats 8). By relative frequency: Section A, 8/20 = 40%; Section B, 15/50 = 30%. Section A is the more favorable group, even though it contributed fewer students.

GroupStudentsPrefer onlineRelative frequency
Section A2088/20 = 40%
Section B501515/50 = 30%
When groups differ in size, comparing frequencies compares the sizes of the groups as much as their opinions. Relative frequencies put every group on the same footing, and reporting which denominator you used is part of telling the truth about a table.
