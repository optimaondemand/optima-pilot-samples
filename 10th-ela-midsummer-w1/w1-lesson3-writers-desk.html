Six Words and a Loanword

Tap a card to see its sense, its root, and the line from the scene.

A right held by one person or group by law or custom.

“I beg the ancient privilege of Athens:” (1.1)

Root Latin privilegium, a law for one person, from privus (“private”) + lex (“law”).

To lessen the force of something; to soften.

“Which by no means we may extenuate” (1.1)

Root Latin extenuare, “to make thin”, from ex- + tenuis (“thin”).

To swear off; to renounce on oath.

“Either to die the death, or to abjure / For ever the society of men.” (1.1)

Root Latin abjurare, “to deny on oath”, from ab- (“away”) + jurare (“swear”).

An order issued by authority; a decree.

“It stands as an edict in destiny.” (1.1)

Root Latin edictum, “a proclamation”, from edicere (“to declare”).

Supreme authority over a person or a realm.

“My soul consents not to give sovereignty.” (1.1)

Root Old French soverain, from Latin super (“above”).

Agreement given freely.

“This man hath my consent to marry her.” (1.1)

Root Latin consentire, “to feel together”, from com- (“together”) + sentire (“feel”).

The list of characters in a play; by extension, any cast of people in a situation.

A modern edition opens with the dramatis personae, so a reader can tell Egeus from Theseus before the first scene begins.

Root Latin, “persons of the drama”, borrowed whole into English.

Four Lines of Verse

Your task
  1. Name the conflict in one sentence: a rule your family keeps, such as a curfew, a planned career, or a rule about friends, set against a choice you want to make. It can be invented.
  2. Draft 4 to 6 lines of unrhymed verse. Aim for about ten syllables and five beats a line. Say each line aloud and tap out the beats.
  3. Make one line inverted. Put its words in an unusual order for emphasis, as Egeus does below.
  4. Write the note. Copy the line where the meter bends on purpose, say where the bend falls, and explain what it makes a reader notice.
  5. Use two words from Word Study, accurately.
