Tools for Everyone

A great tool serves every hand that reaches for it. How?

Adaptive technologies

Adaptive technologies (also called assistive technologies) are tools that help people interact with computers in the way that works best for them. They are not rare extras — many people use them every day, and you have probably used some without naming them:

  • Screen readers read aloud what is on the screen for someone who cannot see it well.
  • Captions and transcripts turn speech into text for someone who is deaf or in a noisy room.
  • Voice control lets a person command a device by speaking.
  • High-contrast modes and text zoom make screens readable for more eyes.
  • Switch access and alternative keyboards let people with limited movement operate a computer.

Adaptive technologies assist users in daily life — at school, at work, at home — by removing barriers between the person and the task. They are also a reason we build our own course materials carefully: clear headings, image descriptions, captions, and keyboard control are how we make sure the work serves everyone. (You will notice our interactives can be used entirely with the keyboard.)

Anchor — Engelbart's "Mother of All Demos" (1968) Watch the assigned clips on your own. In 1968, Douglas Engelbart showed a roomful of people something they had never seen: a mouse moving a pointer, words editing on a screen, two people working in the same document from different places. He was not chiefly interested in the machine — he wanted to augment human intellect: to help people think and work together better.
