Hardware & Software

A machine is metal and silicon. What makes it do anything?

Two halves of one whole

Every computer is the partnership of two things:

  • Hardware — the physical parts you could touch: the processor, memory, storage, the screen, the keyboard. Hardware can do a great deal, but on its own it does nothing — it waits for instructions.
  • Software — the instructions themselves: the programs that tell the hardware what to do, step by step. Software cannot do anything alone either; it needs hardware to run on.

Neither is useful without the other. This partnership is the heart of today's lesson: to describe the relationship between hardware and software.

Waking a computer: firmware, BIOS, and the operating system

When you press the power button, the hardware cannot simply "start working" — it needs instructions immediately. Those first instructions are a special kind of software:

  • Firmware — software stored permanently on a chip inside the hardware. It is the bridge between pure hardware and ordinary software.
  • BIOS (Basic Input/Output System) — firmware that powers up the machine, checks that the hardware is healthy, and begins the start-up process.
  • Operating system (OS) — the large piece of software that then takes charge: it manages all the hardware and other software and gives you something to work with.

So the order matters: firmware/BIOS → operating system → the applications you use. Hardware needed software before it could come alive.

Unplugged Be the Computer Lay out five number cards on your desk and write a tiny program — a few numbered lines for sorting them. Then be the machine: read one line at a time and do exactly what it says. Your hands are the CPU, a sticky note you may glance at is RAM, and a folder set aside is storage. Feel how software (the program) directs hardware (the parts you played), and how nothing happens until an instruction is given.
