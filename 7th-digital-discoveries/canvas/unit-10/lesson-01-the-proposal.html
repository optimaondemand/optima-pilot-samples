Unit 10 · Lesson 1

Vague, but Exciting

A memo, a scrawled note — and the Web was born.

The proposal

Berners-Lee's 1989 memo solved a real problem: CERN's thousands of researchers kept losing each other's information. His idea joined two old friends of ours — documents and links (remember Vannevar Bush's memex from Unit 2?) — into one system anyone could join: pages, connected by hyperlinks, readable by any computer. He did not patent it. He gave it away, and the whole world built on it — including this course, served to you over his invention.

How a webpage works

Every webpage you have ever seen — including this one — is a text file written in HTML (HyperText Markup Language). HTML wraps content in tags that say what each piece is:

<h1>My Rabbit Meadow Findings</h1> <p>This year I tested a hypothesis about food and population.</p> <img src="meadow-chart.png" alt="Chart: the herd settling at 20 rabbits"> <a href="project.html">See the full investigation</a>

A heading, a paragraph, an image (with alt text for screen readers — accessibility is built into good HTML, as we learned in Unit 1), and a link. The browser reads the tags and draws the page. Design tools like Google Sites write the HTML for you — but a maker who can read it is never at the tool's mercy.
