Weaving the Web

Essential question: How do I present what I have made so it is good, true, and beautiful?

In 1989, a young engineer at CERN named Tim Berners-Lee wrote a modest memo proposing a system of linked documents. His boss scrawled on it: "Vague, but exciting." That memo became the World Wide Web — the very fabric this course has traveled all year. In this final unit you learn how the web works, how to create online content with real design tools, and then you do what every thinker in our canon did: you publish. Your capstone is a digital portfolio — the year's best work, gathered, polished, and presented in your own recorded voice.

Anchor text — our final great book Tim Berners-Lee, "Information Management: A Proposal" (CERN, 1989) — w3.org/History/1989/proposal.html. The Web's birth certificate, free to read at the address it made possible.

What we will do

LessonWe will learn to…
1 · Vague, but ExcitingRead the Web's founding memo — and see how a webpage actually works.
2 · The Craftsman's ToolsCreate online content with design tools — and design it well for every reader.
3 · Gathering the YearCurate your digital portfolio: choose, arrange, and introduce your best work.
4 · Polish & PeersRefine with peer feedback, and look once more at where these skills lead.
5 · The PresentationRecord your capstone presentation and close the year with a final reflection.
Our virtue this unit — Courage & service Presenting your own work takes courage; honoring your classmates' work is service. The year's last question is its first: was what we made good, true, and beautiful?

How you will be assessed

A hands-on activity in each lesson, an asynchronous discussion board, a short Unit 10 quiz, one final student-built Arthur VR project — and the Capstone Portfolio itself, the largest single grade of the year, counted in the Projects portion of your grade.
