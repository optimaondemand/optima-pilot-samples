Module 7 Project: Design a Rose Window

Driving question

What makes a circular window look perfectly balanced, and how can you prove that it is?

Objectives
  • Study real circular designs and name what creates their balance.
  • Construct a regular polygon inscribed in a circle, exactly, with a compass and a straightedge.
  • Build two families of congruent chords and at least one tangent line or tangent circle.
  • Find and label central angles, inscribed angles and arcs in your own window.
  • Justify three circle relationships with a theorem and your own numbers.

A

What You Will Make

  1. A circle with an inscribed regular polygon. Draw your window’s circle with a compass. Inside it, construct a regular polygon exactly, with a compass and a straightedge: a hexagon, a square, an octagon or a 12-sided figure. You do not measure the sides to place them.
  2. Two families of congruent chords. The polygon’s sides are the first family. Add a second family, such as the chords that skip a corner. Every chord in a family has the same length.
  3. A tangent. At least one tangent line, or one tangent circle, such as the circle that touches every side of your polygon.
  4. Labeled angles and arcs. At least one central angle and one inscribed angle, each marked with its arc and its measure.
  5. A geometry key. Typed on this page: every part labeled, every angle and arc measure, and three relationships that you justify with a theorem and your own numbers.

Your window must be original. Study real designs first, then make choices that differ from them, and be ready to say why. More than one design can be balanced; yours has to be proved.

From Parts of a Circle

The Parts of a Circle

  • All radii of a circle are equal. A diameter is twice a radius.
  • A chord has both endpoints on the circle. The diameter is the longest chord.
  • A central angle has its vertex at the center, with radii as sides. An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle, with chords as sides.
  • A minor arc is less than 180°. A major arc is more than 180°.
ExampleA circle has diameter 14 cm. Its radius is 14 ÷ 2 = 7 cm, and no chord in it is longer than 14 cm.
Check yourself

A window circle has diameter 18 cm. What is its radius, in centimeters?

Which angle has its vertex on the circle, with two chords as its sides?

From Radii, Chords, and Tangents

Tangents, Chords and the Center

  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency. The converse holds too: a line perpendicular to a radius at its end on the circle is tangent.
  • A diameter perpendicular to a chord cuts the chord in half. So the line from the center perpendicular to a chord lands on the chord’s midpoint.
  • Two chords that cross inside a circle: the product of the two pieces of one chord equals the product of the two pieces of the other.
ExampleA tangent touches a circle of radius 5 at T, and P is 13 from the center O. The angle at T is 90°, so PT = √132 − 52 = 12.
Check yourself

A tangent touches a circle of radius 6 cm at T. Point P is 10 cm from the center. How long is PT, in centimeters?

Chords AB and CD cross at X. AX = 4, XB = 9 and CX = 6. How long is XD?

A chord 16 cm long sits in a circle of radius 10 cm. How far is the chord from the center, in centimeters?

From Secants and Tangents

Two Tangents From One Point

  • Two tangent segments drawn to a circle from the same outside point are equal.
  • A tangent and a secant from one outside point: the tangent squared equals the outside piece of the secant times the whole secant.
ExampleA tangent of length 6, and a secant whose outside piece is 4: 62 = 36 = 4 × 9, so the whole secant is 9.
Check yourself

Tangent segments PA and PB are drawn from the same point P. PA = 3x + 2 and PB = 5x − 6. Find x.

From Constructing a Tangent

Perpendicular Bisectors and Tangents

  • To bisect a segment, open the compass wider than half of it. Swing equal arcs from both ends. The line through the two crossings is the perpendicular bisector.
  • Tangent at a point T on the circle: draw the line through the center O and T. Swing equal arcs from T to cut that line at U and V. From U and V swing wider equal arcs that cross at W. Line TW is perpendicular to the radius at T, so it is tangent.
Check yourself

In the tangent construction, why do the first two arcs, both centered at T, use one compass width?

New for this project

Arcs, Central Angles and Inscribed Angles

  • The whole circle is 360°. A central angle equals its intercepted arc.
  • An inscribed angle is half its intercepted arc. Inscribed angles that intercept the same arc are equal.
  • Adjacent arcs add. The arc formed by two neighbors has the sum of their measures.
ExampleArc NP is 66°. A central angle on it is 66°, and an inscribed angle on it is 66 ÷ 2 = 33°.
Check yourself

Two adjacent arcs measure 84° and 123°. How many degrees is the arc they form together?

An inscribed angle intercepts an arc of 106°. How many degrees is the angle?

New for this project

Regular Polygons in a Circle

  • A regular n-gon inscribed in a circle has n equal sides. Its central angle is 360° ÷ n, and each side cuts off an arc of that size.
  • For a hexagon the central angle is 60°. The triangle formed by the center and one side is isosceles with a 60° apex, so its base angles are 60° too. It is equilateral, so each side equals the radius.
  • Build a hexagon by stepping the radius around the circle: six steps return to the start. Build a square with two perpendicular diameters. Bisect each arc of a square or hexagon to double the corners, to 8 or 12.
  • The perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the center and meets the circle at the midpoint of the chord’s arc. That is why bisecting works.
ExampleAn octagon: 360 ÷ 8 = 45° per central angle. A hexagon in a circle of radius 5 cm has sides of 5 cm.
Check yourself

A regular polygon with 10 sides is inscribed in a circle. How many degrees is each central angle?

New for this project

Equal Chords, Equal Arcs

  • In one circle, equal central angles give equal arcs, and equal arcs give equal chords. The reverse holds too.
  • Why the chords match: each chord and its two radii make a triangle. Two triangles with two equal radii and the same angle between them are congruent, so the chords are equal.
ExampleTwo chords each cut off a 70° arc in the same circle, so the two chords are equal in length.
Check yourself

In one circle, arc PQ and arc RS each measure 50°. What follows?

New for this project

Angles Made by Tangents and Chords

  • Tangent-chord angle: where a tangent and a chord meet at the point of tangency, the angle is half the intercepted arc.
  • Two tangents meeting outside the circle: the angle is half the difference of the two arcs, larger minus smaller.
  • Two chords crossing inside: the angle is half the sum of the arcs it and its vertical angle intercept.
ExampleIn a hexagon window, the tangent at a corner and the side from that corner meet at ½(60°) = 30°. Tangents at two neighboring corners meet at ½(300° − 60°) = 120°.
Check yourself

A tangent and a chord meet at the point of tangency and intercept an arc of 130°. How many degrees is the angle between them?

Two tangents meet outside a circle and intercept arcs of 242° and 118°. How many degrees is the angle between the tangents?

New for this project

Triangles and Quadrilaterals Inscribed in a Circle

  • A triangle inscribed in a circle with a diameter as one side is a right triangle. The right angle is opposite the diameter.
  • Opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral add to 180°.
ExampleAn inscribed quadrilateral with a 75° angle has an opposite angle of 180 − 75 = 105°.
Check yourself

An inscribed quadrilateral has an angle of 107°. How many degrees is the angle opposite it?

Triangle ABC is inscribed in a circle and AB is a diameter. How many degrees is angle C?

GROUP 1

The Circle and the Polygon

  1. Draw the circle.

    Mark a light dot for the center, O. Set the compass opening to your radius R with the ruler, then draw the full circle. Keep the small hole the compass point leaves at O.

  2. Draw a diameter.

    With the straightedge, draw a light line through O that crosses the circle in two points. Call the top point P and the bottom point Q.

  3. Step the radius around.

    Keep the compass at R. Put the point on P and swing a short arc across the circle. Move the point to that new mark and swing again. Keep going until you have six marks. The third mark should land on Q, and the sixth back on P.

    After six arcs, your last mark misses P by 3 mm. The tolerance is 2 mm. What is the best next move?

  4. Build the perpendicular diameter.

    Open the compass wider than R. Put the point on P and swing arcs on both sides of O. Keep the same opening and swing arcs from Q. The arcs cross in two points. Draw the line through those crossings. It runs through O and meets the circle at S and T. Now P, S, Q and T are four equal steps around the circle.

    Two perpendicular diameters meet at the center. How many degrees is the central angle between neighboring end points?

  5. Double the corners.

    Take any two neighboring marks. Open the compass wider than half the gap between them, and swing equal arcs from both so they cross at a point X. Draw line OX and extend it to the circle. It meets the circle at the middle of that arc. Mark it. Do this for every pair of neighbors.

    Why it works: O and X are both the same distance from the two marks, so line OX is their perpendicular bisector, and a bisector through the center cuts the arc in half.

    A square has 4 marks on the circle. You bisect each of its 4 arcs. How many marks are on the circle now?

    A hexagon has 6 marks on the circle. You bisect each of its 6 arcs. How many marks are on the circle now?

  6. Join and label.

    Join neighboring marks, in order, with the straightedge. Label the corners A, B, C and so on, clockwise from the top. Your polygon is regular: every side is a chord, and all the chords are equal.

GROUP 2

Two Families of Chords

  1. Draw the second family.

    Join each corner to the corner two places along: A to C, B to D, and so on around the circle. Use light lines. These chords skip one corner. With a square, this family is just the two diameters.

  2. Check that each family is equal.

    Find your side length and your skip-one chord length in the table in Plan It. Measure two chords from each family with the ruler. Chords in a family should agree within 2 mm. Write which family is which on your drawing.

    In an 8-sided window, a chord skips one corner. How many degrees is the arc it cuts off?

    Optional: add a third family that skips two corners, for a richer pattern.

GROUP 3

A Tangent

  1. Choose your tangent. Build at least one.

    • A. A tangent line at one corner.
    • B. A tangent circle that touches every side of the polygon.
    • C. Tangent lines at every corner. They meet outside the circle and form an outer ring.

  2. Choice A
    Tangent line at a corner.

    Pick a corner and call it T. Draw the line through O and T, extended past T. With the compass point on T, swing equal arcs that cut this line at U (outside) and V (inside). Open the compass wider than TU. From U and then from V, swing equal arcs that cross at W. Draw line TW. It is perpendicular to the radius at T, so it is tangent.

    A tangent and a radius meet at the point of tangency. How many degrees is the angle between them?

  3. Choice B
    Tangent circle.

    Pick one side of your polygon. Open the compass wider than half the side and swing equal arcs from both ends so they cross. The line through the crossing and O meets the side at its midpoint, M. Put the compass point on O and the pencil on M, and draw the circle. It touches every side once.

    A chord 12 cm long sits in a circle of radius 10 cm. A circle with the same center touches that chord at its midpoint. What is that circle’s radius, in centimeters?

    Why is each side of your polygon tangent to the circle centered at O that passes through the side’s midpoint?

  4. Choice C, optional
    Tangents at every corner.

    Repeat the Choice A construction at each corner. Neighboring tangent lines meet outside the circle. From each meeting point the two tangent segments are equal.

GROUP 4

Central Angles, Inscribed Angles and Arcs

  1. Mark a central angle.

    Pick two neighboring corners, B and C. Draw the radii OB and OC in a darker line. Mark the angle at O in blue and the arc BC in red. Write the measure of each from the tiles in Plan It.

  2. Mark an inscribed angle.

    Choose a corner D on the far side of the circle from B and C. Draw the chords DB and DC. Mark the angle at D in blue and write its measure. It should be half of arc BC.

    In a 12-sided window, an arc covers three sides. How many degrees is its central angle?

    An inscribed angle intercepts that same arc. How many degrees is the inscribed angle?

  3. Add one more pair.

    Mark a second central angle and inscribed angle on a different arc, so your drawing shows two pairs. A tangent and a chord from the same corner give a third angle, and it should equal half its arc.

  4. Check with a protractor.

    Read one central angle you marked. A protractor checks an angle but never builds one.

    The exact central angle is 60°. Your protractor reads 62.5°. The tolerance is 2°. Is your angle within tolerance?

GROUP 5

Finish the Drawing

  1. Darken and clean up.

    Trace the circle, polygon, chords and tangents in a darker, even line. Leave every construction arc and helper line in light pencil. They are your proof, so do not erase them.

  2. Label and color.

    Letter every corner, mark O, the points of tangency, and each angle and arc measure. Use blue for construction and red for results, or two colors of your choice. Wipe off smudges and keep the page clean.

  3. Photograph it.

    Take one photo of the whole drawing from straight above in good light, with the whole circle in the frame. Take a second photo close up of your construction marks.

GROUP 6

The Geometry Key

  1. Label every part and give every measure.

    Name each labeled part: the center, a radius, the corners, each chord family, your tangent line or circle and its point of tangency. Then list every central angle, inscribed angle and arc you marked, with its measure and the arithmetic behind it. Check that the arcs around the circle add to 360°.

  2. Justify three relationships.

    Choose three from this list, or one of your own that shows up in your window. For each one, state it, point to it on your drawing with letters, name the theorem, and use your window’s own numbers.

    1. Equal chords cut off equal arcs.
    2. A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency.
    3. An inscribed angle is half its central angle on the same arc.
    4. Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal.
    5. An angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90°.
    6. Opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral add to 180°.
    7. Two tangent segments from one point are equal.
    8. The line from the center perpendicular to a chord bisects it.
