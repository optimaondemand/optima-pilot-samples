Module 6 Project: Three Witnesses, One Story

Focus

How can several independent lines of evidence, gathered by different scientists with different methods, add up to one strong case for where living things come from?

Objectives
  • Choose at least three independent lines of evidence and support each with a specific named example.
  • Explain why each line points to common descent, and why independent lines that agree make the case stronger than any one line alone.
  • Build one connected argument of about 400 to 700 words that addresses homologous versus analogous structures and ties to variation and classification.

Your Task

The task

Evaluate at least three independent lines of evidence for evolution and construct a single, unified argument for common descent, explaining not only what each line shows, but why several independent lines pointing to the same conclusion make the case so strong.

This is the writing that pulls the module together. You studied the evidence for evolution (the fossil record, comparative anatomy, biogeography, and molecular sequences), the mechanisms that drive it (natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, mutation), and how populations split into new species. Now you will do what Darwin did: gather independent observations and build them into a single argument.

Your task is to choose at least three independent lines of evidence and weave them into one reasoned case for common descent: the idea that today’s species are descended, with modification, from shared ancestors. Your teacher reads and scores this one personally.

Open inquiry

Unlike the more guided essays earlier in the year, this one leaves the construction to you. You choose which three (or more) lines of evidence to argue from, you decide the order, and you build the reasoning that ties them into one conclusion. The evidence bank is a memory aid, not a script: a strong essay selects from it and explains, in its own words, why the evidence points where it does.

There is no single “correct” set of three. There is only an argument that is well reasoned or one that is not.

B

Two Words to Keep Apart

Common descent is the claim that living species share ancestors. Natural selection is one mechanism that drives the change. Your argument is about the first: use the mechanisms as support, but keep your claim about shared ancestry.

Common descent = shared ancestry · Natural selection = one mechanism

My Three Witnesses

Write in connected paragraphs, not a list. The wording is yours. These are the moves a strong argument makes, not a fill-in-the-blank.

1

Claim

State, in one or two sentences, that the species alive today share common ancestors, and that several independent lines of evidence converge to support this. Make it a real claim you intend to defend, not a definition copied from the bank.

2

Evidence

Present your three (or more) chosen lines, each with a specific example. Not “fossils show evolution,” but a named transitional fossil, a named homologous structure, a real biogeographic pattern, or a sequence-similarity comparison. Specificity is what separates an argument from an opinion.

My witnesses
3

Reasoning

This is the heart of the essay. For each line, explain why it points to common descent. For example, why do the same bones, rearranged for different jobs, suggest a shared ancestor rather than coincidence?

Then make the move that matters most: explain why independent lines, collected by different methods with no reason to agree by chance, arriving at the same conclusion makes that conclusion far stronger than any single line alone.

As a check on your reasoning, address one honest counter-consideration. How is a homologous structure different from an analogous one, and why does that distinction strengthen rather than weaken your case?

Homologous
Evidence of relatednessThe same forelimb bones in a human arm, whale flipper, and bat wing.
Analogous
Similar function, separate originsA bird’s wing and an insect’s wing.
4

Integration

Close by connecting your argument to the module’s bigger story. The variation evolution works on comes from meiosis and mutation. The relationships your evidence reveals are exactly what classification organizes into the tree of life.

Courage

You may also reflect on the virtue behind this project, courage: the courage to follow evidence wherever it leads, even into controversy. Darwin spent some twenty years gathering evidence and anticipating objections before he published. He assembled independent lines of evidence and answered objections before his critics could raise them.

Choosing your evidence deliberately, reasoning honestly about what it does and does not show, and facing the hardest distinction (homologous versus analogous) rather than stepping around it is the same patient, evidence-first habit.
