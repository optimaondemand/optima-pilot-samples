Module 8 Project: The Twelfth Root of Two

Driving question

Why does a piano sound almost, but not quite, in tune, and which tuning is “right”?

Objectives
  • Rewrite a power with a fractional exponent as a radical, and the reverse, and reduce the exponent.
  • Use the laws of exponents and the properties of radicals to combine, simplify and compare multipliers such as 27/12.
  • Model a length that is inversely proportional to a frequency, and compute it to the nearest tenth of a millimeter.
  • Build and measure a one-octave instrument and judge its accuracy against your computed lengths.
  • Argue for a choice that has more than one defensible answer, using numbers as evidence.

Some intervals come out closer to a whole-number ratio than others, and some land closer in one tuning than in the other. You will find out which ones. Nobody can hand you the answer: your choice has to stand on numbers you computed and measured, and you have to say what it costs.

Rational Exponents

From Rational Exponents
The fraction in an exponent is a root and a power. The bottom number is the root and the top number is the power. xmn = n√xm
  • Same base, multiplying: add the exponents. Same base, dividing: subtract them.
  • A power raised to a power: multiply the exponents.
  • Reduce a fraction exponent like any fraction.
ExampleWrite 2712 as a radical, and write 2612 in simplest form.
  1. The denominator 12 is the root and the numerator 7 is the power on the 2: 12√27.
  2. For the second one, reduce the exponent first. Divide 6 and 12 by 6 to get one half.
  3. An exponent of one half is a square root: 2612 = 212 = √2.

From The Number e

The number e is an irrational constant, about 2.71828. It shows up wherever growth is continuous, such as continuously compounded interest. Your project uses base 2, but what carries over is this: the laws of exponents work the same way for every base, e included.

Radical Expressions

From Radical Expressions
A radical is a root. It is simplified when no perfect power of its index is left under it. For nonnegative a and b, the root of a product is the product of the roots. √ab = √a · √b
ExampleSimplify √72.
  1. Look for the largest perfect square that divides 72. It is 36, because 72 = 36 · 2.
  2. Split the root: √36 · 2 = √36 · √2.
  3. The square root of 36 is 6. The answer is 6√2.

In this project, 2412 reduces to 213, which is the cube root of 2.

D

Adding and Subtracting Radicals

From Adding and Subtracting Radical Expressions
Only like radicals combine. Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand. Add or subtract their coefficients and keep the radical. If the radicals look unlike, simplify each one first. 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2
ExampleSimplify √8 + √2.
  1. The radicands differ, so simplify first. √8 = √4 · 2 = 2√2.
  2. Now both terms have √2. A plain √2 has a coefficient of 1.
  3. Add the coefficients: 2 + 1 = 3. The answer is 3√2.

1Simplify √18 + √2.

2Two pipes, each 6 semitones above the first note of its own set, need 100√2 mm and 50√2 mm of straw. The total is ?√2 mm. Type the number that replaces ?.

E

Multiplying and Dividing Radicals

Needed for the lengths in this project
Radicals with the same index multiply and divide under one root. The radicands do not have to match. After combining, simplify. √a · √b = √ab √a ÷ √b = √a ÷ b

Standard form leaves no radical in a denominator. To rationalize the denominator, multiply the top and the bottom by the radical that is in the bottom. This gives a perfect square there.

ExampleA pipe is 100 ÷ √2 mm long. Rewrite it with no radical in the denominator.
  1. Multiply the top and the bottom by √2. This does not change the value, because it multiplies by 1.
  2. The bottom becomes √2 · √2 = √4 = 2. The top becomes 100√2.
  3. Reduce 100 over 2. The answer is 50√2 mm.

1Simplify: √2 · √8

2Which equals 1√2 with no radical in the denominator?

3Simplify: 3√2 · 3√2 · 3√2

7

Cut and Label

Straw pan pipes

  1. Lay a straw flat with its open end at the 0 mark of your ruler. Mark the cut length from your cut list, in millimeters.
  2. Cut about 2 mm long, then trim to the mark. A pipe can be made shorter but never longer.
  3. Seal the other end with a small piece of tape folded flat, so no air leaks. The length is measured from the open top to the sealed end.
  4. Write the note number and the length on each pipe.
  5. Tape the pipes side by side on a strip of paper or cardboard, longest to shortest, with the open tops level.

One-string monochord

  1. Stretch the string tight between two fixed points exactly L mm apart. Measure with your ruler, and keep the same tension throughout.
  2. Starting at the left fixed point, measure each cut length along the string and mark it on the board with a pencil.
  3. Write the note number and the length beside each mark.
  4. To play a note, press the string firmly to the board at its mark and pluck the part between the mark and the left fixed point.
8

Play and Check

Play the eight notes in order from the longest length to the shortest. They should rise in steps. If one note sounds out of place, measure it again before you change anything. A shorter pipe or string sounds higher, so a note that is too low is too long. If you have a keyboard or a recording, compare your fifth and your major third to it.
