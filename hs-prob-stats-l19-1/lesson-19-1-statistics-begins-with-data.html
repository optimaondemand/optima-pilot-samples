Statistics is the science of learning from data

Every claim you have heard this month that begins “studies show” or “most people prefer” rests on one thing: somebody collected data. Statistics is the discipline that asks how to collect it, how to display it, and how far it can honestly be trusted. Semester 1 taught you to reason about chance when the rules of the game are known: a fair coin, a well-shuffled deck. Semester 2 turns the problem around. Now the rules are not known; all we have is what we observed, and we must reason backward from it.

Honesty is this module's virtue, and it begins in a very plain place: saying exactly what was measured, on whom, and in what units. A graph can be drawn beautifully and still deceive if nobody can tell what its numbers mean. So before we draw anything, we learn the vocabulary of data itself.

The words

The individuals are the people, animals, or things we collect information about: students, trees, batteries, cities. A variable is a characteristic that can take different values from one individual to the next: a student's favorite subject, a tree's height, a city's population. Data are the recorded values of the variables for the individuals we examined.

The words

The population is the entire group we want to learn about. A sample is the part of the population we actually examine. Asking every member of the population is a census; asking 60 of them gives a sample of size n = 60. The price of sampling is that we must be honest about the difference between “what the sample showed” and “what the whole population is like.” Module 11 is devoted to doing that well.

The Two Kinds of Variable

Time≈ 15 min
Status—

Variables come in two families, and which family a variable belongs to decides which displays and calculations make sense.

Categorical variables place each individual into a group or category: favorite subject, rides the bus (yes/no), eye color. Counting how many fall in each category makes sense; averaging them does not. Numerical variables are measurements or counts for which arithmetic is meaningful: hours of sleep, number of siblings, height. Numerical variables split once more: discrete variables are counted and take separated values (0, 1, 2, 3 siblings), while continuous variables are measured and can take any value in an interval (7.5 hours, 6.25 hours, 7.3333 hours…).

Numbers that are really labels

A number written down is not automatically a numerical variable. A locker number is a label: locker 407 is not “twice” locker 214 in any way that matters, and the average of two locker numbers means nothing. Jersey numbers, zip codes, and student ID numbers are the same: categorical variables that happen to use digits. The test is simple: would adding or averaging these values tell you anything? If not, the variable is categorical.

One Variable or Two?

Time≈ 10 min
Status—

Data that records one variable for each individual is univariate (“one variable”): the list of everyone's hours of sleep. Data that records two variables for each individual is bivariate: each student's hours of sleep and whether they ride the bus. Bivariate data lets us ask whether two variables are associated: whether knowing one tells us something about the other.

Bivariate data comes in three flavors, each with its own display that you will meet in this module and in Module 10.

Sketches only: no real data.

Carry this the whole semester: even a strong association between two variables does not by itself show that one causes the other. Seeing a pattern is the first step; explaining it honestly is a much longer one.

A moment in the history of mathematics

John Graunt (1620–1674) was a London merchant, not a university mathematician. In 1662 he published Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality. The “bills” were weekly lists, kept by London parishes, of burials and christenings with the reported causes of death: a pile of records that most people saw only as a grim list of individual losses. Graunt did something new: he gathered years of bills, sorted the entries into categories, and counted.

As the story is usually told, the counts revealed regularities no single entry could show: the number of deaths from many causes stayed strikingly steady from year to year, and slightly more boys than girls were christened. He also questioned how reliable the recorded causes were, which is exactly the honest habit this module is about. Graunt is often credited as an early pioneer of the idea that sorting and counting data can reveal the shape of a population. That is where we begin: with categories, counts, and the care to ask where the numbers came from.
