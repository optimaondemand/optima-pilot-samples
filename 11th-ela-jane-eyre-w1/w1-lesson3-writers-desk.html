Write Three

  1. A periodic sentence. At least three conditions, times, or details first, and the main clause last.
  2. A cumulative sentence. The main clause in the first five words, then three additions.
  3. A short blow. Eight words or fewer, set directly after your periodic sentence so that it lands.

Ten Words

Tap a word to open its card.

opprobrium

noun

Public disgrace and blame heaped on someone. Here it is the scorn of a whole household.

“My head still ached and bled with the blow and fall I had received: no one had reproved John for wantonly striking me; and because I had turned against him to avert farther irrational violence, I was loaded with general opprobrium.” Ch. II

Origin Latin opprobrium, ‘reproach, disgrace’, from ob- (‘against’) + probrum (‘a shameful act, a reproach’).

sanguine

adjective

Cheerful and confident by nature. In Brontë’s list it also means warm and high-spirited.

“I know that had I been a sanguine, brilliant, careless, exacting, handsome, romping child · · ·” Ch. II

Origin Latin sanguineus, ‘of blood’, from sanguis, ‘blood’. Older medicine held that a body rich in blood made a person warm and cheerful.

interloper

noun

Someone who pushes in where they do not belong and are not wanted. Here “race” means family line: Jane is not a Reed by blood.

“· · · how could she really like an interloper not of her race, and unconnected with her, after her husband’s death, by any tie?” Ch. II

Origin English, about 1600: inter- (‘between’) + loper, from Dutch lopen (‘to run’). It first named a trader who ran in on a business he had no licence for.

consternation

noun

Sudden alarm that leaves the mind in confusion.

“What a consternation of soul was mine that dreary afternoon!” Ch. II

Origin Latin consternare, ‘to alarm, to throw into confusion’.

duplicity

noun

Deceit: saying or seeming one thing while meaning another. Here it is the charge Mrs. Reed believes.

“I was a precocious actress in her eyes; she sincerely looked on me as a compound of virulent passions, mean spirit, and dangerous duplicity.” Ch. II

Origin Latin duplicitas, from duplex, ‘twofold’ (duo, ‘two’ + plicare, ‘to fold’): a two-faced manner.

antipathy

noun

A deep, settled dislike.

“John had not much affection for his mother and sisters, and an antipathy to me.” Ch. I

Origin Greek antipatheia, from anti- (‘against’) + pathos (‘feeling’).

vassalage

noun

A following of dependents who serve a powerful person. Here it is the household around Mrs. Reed.

“I was a discord in Gateshead Hall: I was like nobody there; I had nothing in harmony with Mrs. Reed or her children, or her chosen vassalage.” Ch. II

Origin From vassal, a servant bound to a lord: Old French, from Medieval Latin vassallus, which came from a Celtic word for ‘servant’.

impudence

noun

Rude boldness toward someone who deserves respect. John Reed names Jane’s answer this way.

“That is for your impudence in answering mama awhile since,” Ch. I

Origin Latin impudens, ‘shameless’, from in- (‘not’) + pudens (‘feeling shame’).

precocious

adjective

Developed early; ahead of one’s years. Here it describes a power of reasoning that comes early and does not last.

“· · · forced by the agonising stimulus into precocious though transitory power · · ·” Ch. II

Origin Latin praecox, ‘ripening early’, from prae- (‘before’) + coquere (‘to cook, to ripen’).

insupportable

adjective

Too much to bear; unbearable.

“· · · some strange expedient to achieve escape from insupportable oppression · · ·” Ch. II

Origin Late Latin insupportabilis, ‘not able to be carried’: in- (‘not’) + supportare (‘to carry, to bring’).
