Six Words and a Loanword

Each word comes from Part I. Translations differ, so a word may not appear in your copy, but each card names the moment it belongs to.

vermin

noun

Small creatures regarded as pests; also a contemptuous word for people.

The inspector would not let the grain be sold until the vermin in the warehouse were cleared out.

Root Latin vermis, “worm,” through Old French vermine.

Where The opening of Part I, where the narration names what Gregor has become. The word varies by translation.

strenuous

adjective

Requiring great effort.

Climbing the hill with a full pack was a strenuous way to spend an afternoon.

Root Latin strenuus, “vigorous, active.”

Where Gregor’s first complaints about the traveling career.

subordinate

noun

A person who works under another’s authority.

The manager praised her subordinates in public but decided everything herself.

Root Latin sub, “under,” + ordinare, “to arrange,” from ordo, “order.”

Where Gregor’s thoughts about the boss at his desk and how he deals with the men beneath him.

suspicious

adjective

Inclined to distrust; showing distrust.

The guard grew suspicious when the same man asked about the vault three days running.

Root Latin suspicere, “to look up at” and so “to mistrust,” from sub + specere, “to look.”

Where Gregor’s thoughts about the firm sending the chief clerk to the flat.

diligence

noun

Steady, careful effort.

Her diligence showed in the neat columns of figures she copied out each night.

Root Latin diligentia, from diligere, “to value, to choose.”

Where Gregor’s long speech to the chief clerk, once the door is open.

retreat

verb, noun

To move back; to withdraw. As a noun, the act of withdrawing.

The crowd began to retreat from the edge of the platform as the water rose.

Root Latin retrahere, “to draw back,” through Old French.

Where The chief clerk backing away at the moment the door opens.

angst

noun · borrowed from German

A deep, general anxiety with no single clear cause.

A vague angst followed her through the day, though nothing had actually gone wrong.

Root German Angst, “fear, anxiety.” English has kept the word as it came.

Where Not a word from Part I, but one from Kafka’s own language, and a name for the mood of the whole story.
