Mr. MagisterYour history teacher

In 1519, Moctezuma met Cortés on a causeway and let the Spaniards into Tenochtitlan. You will judge that choice from the city's own layout.

Your evidence is a map. It was printed in Nuremberg in 1524 and shows the Mexica capital as Europeans first saw it in print. You will pin three places on it where the layout mattered for defending the city or for entering it. At each pin you write what you see and what it made easy or dangerous for a defender or for an invader.

Then you write one paragraph that answers this question, and you cite your pins as evidence.

What does the layout of the city suggest about the choice Moctezuma made, and about how easily it could have gone another way?

A printed map from 1524 is also a source with limits. Your paragraph must say at least one thing the map cannot tell us.

Step 2

Know what this map is

  • Printed in Nuremberg, 1524. A woodcut printed by Friedrich Peypus in a Latin edition of Cortés's second letter to Emperor Charles V.
  • Copied from a drawing. The printer worked from a drawing that came with the letter, not from the city itself.
  • The maker is unknown. The original drawing may have been made by a Mexica artist or by a Spaniard. Historians are not sure.
  • Not drawn with north at the top. The Gulf of Mexico sketch on the same sheet has Florida at the bottom. Use the map's own features to find your way, not compass directions.
  • Labels are in Latin, and some are upside down.
  • A European print. The houses and towers are drawn in a style that looks European, whatever the buildings were really like.
