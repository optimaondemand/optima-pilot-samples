Recitation Q3: Say It From Memory
English 2 · Module 3 · A Midsummer Night’s Dream
20 pointsAudio or video recordingDue in Week 8
What you make
 
Recite one passage of Shakespeare’s verse from memory, aloud, with line endings, stress, and pauses placed for sense. Use any device that records audio or video.
What a recitation is
 
A recitation is a passage learned by heart and delivered aloud without notes. The memory has to hold the words, the order of the lines, and the places where the voice pauses, rises, and drops. Verse helps: the line length, the rhyme, and the rhythm act as supports. Verse also asks for care, because a line break is a place where a speaker may breathe even when the sentence goes on.
Two kinds of speaker appear on the menu. Puck and Helena speak to an audience, openly or privately. Theseus and Titania speak as rulers explaining a view of the world. A recitation brings out which kind of speaker is present.
The passage menu
 
Read all four aloud once before choosing. Choose the speech that is easiest to say with conviction.
APuck’s Epilogue16 lines · 5.1
Puck, 16 lines. The play’s last speech.
If we shadows have offended,
Think but this, and all is mended,
That you have but slumber’d here
While these visions did appear.
And this weak and idle theme,
No more yielding but a dream,
Gentles, do not reprehend.
If you pardon, we will mend.
And, as I am an honest Puck,
If we have unearnèd luck
Now to ’scape the serpent’s tongue,
We will make amends ere long;
Else the Puck a liar call.
So, good night unto you all.
Give me your hands, if we be friends,
And Robin shall restore amends.
VERSE FORMshort rhymed lines of seven or eight syllables, with four beats to a line.
WHY CHOOSE ITa plea to the audience, light in tone and quick in rhythm.
BTheseus on Imagination16 lines · 5.1
Theseus, 16 lines, cut from his speech at the start of Act 5, Scene 1.
The lunatic, the lover, and the poet
Are of imagination all compact:
One sees more devils than vast hell can hold;
That is the madman: the lover, all as frantic,
Sees Helen’s beauty in a brow of Egypt:
The poet’s eye, in a fine frenzy rolling,
Doth glance from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven;
And as imagination bodies forth
The forms of things unknown, the poet’s pen
Turns them to shapes, and gives to airy nothing
A local habitation and a name.
Such tricks hath strong imagination,
That if it would but apprehend some joy,
It comprehends some bringer of that joy.
Or in the night, imagining some fear,
How easy is a bush supposed a bear?
VERSE FORMblank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter), with a long sentence that runs across line breaks.
WHY CHOOSE ITan argument built in three figures that ends on a joke about fear.
CHelena Alone16 lines · 1.1
Helena, 16 lines, cut from her speech alone at the end of Act 1, Scene 1.
How happy some o’er other some can be!
Through Athens I am thought as fair as she.
But what of that? Demetrius thinks not so;
He will not know what all but he do know.
And as he errs, doting on Hermia’s eyes,
So I, admiring of his qualities.
Things base and vile, holding no quantity,
Love can transpose to form and dignity.
Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind;
And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind.
Nor hath love’s mind of any judgment taste.
Wings, and no eyes, figure unheedy haste.
And therefore is love said to be a child,
Because in choice he is so oft beguil’d.
As waggish boys in game themselves forswear,
So the boy Love is perjur’d everywhere.
VERSE FORMrhymed couplets in iambic pentameter, so each pair of lines closes on a rhyme.
WHY CHOOSE ITa speech that reasons, couplet by couplet, about why love is blind.
DTitania on the Quarrel20 lines · 2.1
Titania, 20 lines, cut from a longer speech in Act 2, Scene 1.
These are the forgeries of jealousy:
And never, since the middle summer’s spring,
Met we on hill, in dale, forest, or mead,
By pavèd fountain, or by rushy brook,
Or on the beachèd margent of the sea,
To dance our ringlets to the whistling wind,
But with thy brawls thou hast disturb’d our sport.
Therefore the winds, piping to us in vain,
As in revenge, have suck’d up from the sea
Contagious fogs; which, falling in the land,
Hath every pelting river made so proud
That they have overborne their continents.
The ox hath therefore stretch’d his yoke in vain,
The ploughman lost his sweat, and the green corn
Hath rotted ere his youth attain’d a beard.
The fold stands empty in the drownèd field,
And crows are fatted with the murrion flock;
The nine-men’s-morris is fill’d up with mud,
And the quaint mazes in the wanton green,
For lack of tread, are undistinguishable.
VERSE FORMblank verse, with a catalog of floods, rotted crops, and ruined games that builds in force.
WHY CHOOSE ITa list that grows heavier line by line, ending on what the damage has left unrecognizable.

How to prepare
 
Several weeks of ungraded practice come before the recording. The routine below fits verse.
1Memorize in chunks. Break the passage where the lines break. Master two or three lines, then link them to the next.
2Say it aloud every time. Silent reading builds a different memory from speaking.
3Let punctuation mark the breath. A comma is a short pause, a semicolon a longer one, a colon a pause that points forward. Where a line ends with no mark, carry the voice across the break.
4Eyes up. Look up from the page a little longer each time. By Week 8 the page is not needed.
5Decide what the speech is doing. Puck’s epilogue is an apology, Theseus’s speech is an argument, Helena’s is a reckoning, and Titania’s is an accusation. Pace and emphasis should show which.
Steps
 
1Choose one passage from the menu. Read it through once, then set it aside.
2Mark a copy of the passage: // at each full stop, / at each short pause, and an underline under the word that carries each sentence. Rehearse from memory with the marks in mind, then set the copy aside.
3Find a quiet space and set up a recording device: a phone, a tablet, a computer, or a school-provided tool.
4Recite the passage from memory, once through, with no script or on-screen copy. If the place is lost, pause, breathe, and continue. That is normal and does not disqualify the recording.
5Listen back once. If the pace was rushed or the voice was hard to hear, record again.
6Name the file, or copy the link, and submit the best take.
Done when
 
☐The full passage is recited from memory, without reading from a script during the recording.
☐Pauses fall where the sense and punctuation call for them, and run-on lines are carried through the line break.
☐Stress, pace, and volume fit what the passage is doing.
☐The recording is audible from start to finish and opens when submitted.
How it is scored
 
Accuracy & completeness8 pts
FULL CREDITThe whole passage is recited word for word, from memory.
PARTIALLines are missing, reordered, or changed, or the passage is clearly read from a script.
Meter & pause4 pts
FULL CREDITPauses fall where the sense and punctuation call for them, and run-on lines are carried through the line break.
PARTIALEvery line ends with the same drop in the voice, or pauses fall in the middle of a sentence.
Expression & interpretation4 pts
FULL CREDITStress, pace, and volume reflect what the passage is doing (a plea, an argument, a reckoning, an accusation).
PARTIALDelivery is flat or does not change with the sense of the passage.
Recording quality4 pts
FULL CREDITAudible and clear from start to finish, one continuous take of the passage, and the file or link opens.
PARTIALHard to hear in places, cut off, or the file or link does not open.
Total: 20 points
