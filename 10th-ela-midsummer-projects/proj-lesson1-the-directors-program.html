The Staging Comparison

Egeus brings his complaint to the duke. Here is what the text fixes, and two made-up stagings of it.

Egeus’s complaint (1.1)

Enter Egeus, Hermia, Lysander and Demetrius.

The text fixes
  • The words and their order.
  • Who is on stage: the four named in the direction.
  • That Egeus asks for “the ancient privilege of Athens” against his own daughter.
The text leaves open
  • Where each person stands.
  • Whether Egeus looks at Hermia.
  • How fast he speaks, and where he pauses.
Example staging A · made up for this lesson

Egeus stays beside the duke and speaks to him alone. Hermia stands across the stage, and he never looks at her.

Emphasizes: a formal complaint made in public.

Leaves out: any sign that the accuser is her father.

Example staging B · made up for this lesson

Egeus crosses to Hermia and holds her arm on “As she is mine I may dispose of her”. The court goes still.

Emphasizes: ownership, shown to everyone.

Leaves out: the duke as the one who rules; he becomes a watcher.

Now your own scene. What your staging will do is your plan. The text is the fixed part.

What the text fixes in my scene Words, order, who is on stage, any direction. 2–3 sentences.
What my staging adds One choice you will make that the text does not, and what it emphasizes. 2–3 sentences.
What my staging leaves out What the text carries that your choice puts in the background. 2–3 sentences.
