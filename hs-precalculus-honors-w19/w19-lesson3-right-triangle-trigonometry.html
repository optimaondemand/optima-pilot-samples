Right Triangle Trigonometry

Focus

Sine, cosine and tangent are called functions of an angle. What stops the same angle in two different triangles from giving two different ratios?

Objectives
  • Define sine, cosine and tangent as ratios of the sides of a right triangle.
  • Explain, using similar triangles, why these ratios depend only on the angle and never on the size of the triangle.
  • Find a missing side of a right triangle, given one side and one acute angle.
  • Find a missing acute angle of a right triangle, given two sides, using an inverse trigonometric function.

A Fixed Angle, a Fixed Shape

Hipparchus built his chord table because a fixed angle, he realized, always comes bundled with a fixed relationship between the sides of the triangle it sits inside, no matter how large or small you draw that triangle. Surveyors, sailors and engineers have carried the modern right-triangle version of that insight, three ratios, in their pockets for well over two thousand years.

Here is the claim, stated plainly. Draw a right triangle with one acute angle equal to θ. Measure the ratio of the leg opposite θ to the hypotenuse. Now draw a completely different right triangle, bigger, smaller or rotated, as long as it also has an acute angle of θ. Measure the same ratio. It comes out identical, every time. That single fact is what lets sine, cosine and tangent be genuine functions of an angle alone: whatever triangle you happen to draw disappears from the answer, and only θ survives.

Did you knowThe words “sine” and “cosine” trace back, indirectly, to a translation accident. Hindu mathematicians used a Sanskrit word, jya, for a half-chord in a circle. Arabic translators rendered the sound as jiba, which has no meaning of its own in Arabic but resembles jaib, meaning “bay” or “fold of a garment.” A later Latin translator took jaib literally and wrote sinus, “bay” or “curve,” the word English inherited as “sine.” A misheard word about a half-chord became the name of one of the most-used functions in mathematics.

Common mistakeMixing up which leg is “opposite” when the labeled angle changes. Before writing a single ratio, re-read the triangle from the angle named in the problem: which leg is directly across from it, and which leg forms one of its two sides along with the hypotenuse? Skipping that re-check is the most common reason a sine problem turns into a cosine problem.

Why the Ratios Do Not Care About Size

Two right triangles that share an acute angle θ automatically share two angles: that angle, and the right angle each one carries. By the Angle-Angle (AA) similarity theorem, two triangles that agree on two angles are similar: the same shape, possibly a different size. The defining property of similar triangles is that corresponding sides are all scaled by the same factor, so any ratio of two sides is unaffected by that scaling. It cancels out.

This is why sin θ, cos θ and tan θ deserve to be called functions of θ in the first place: for each value of θ, similar triangles guarantee exactly one value for each ratio, whichever triangle is in front of you.

Example 1Triangle A has legs 3 and 4 with hypotenuse 5. Triangle B has legs 6 and 8 with hypotenuse 10, exactly twice the size. Both share the acute angle θ opposite the shorter leg. Show that sin θ comes out the same for both.
  1. Triangle A: sin θ = 35 = 0.6.
  2. Triangle B: sin θ = 610 = 0.6.
  3. Doubling every side doubled the numerator and the denominator together, and the 2’s cancelled. Both give sin θ = 0.6.
  4. This is not a coincidence about these particular numbers. It is what “similar” always does to a ratio.

Key fact: where tangent comes from. Tangent is not an independent idea. It is built entirely out of sine and cosine:

sin θcos θ = oppositehypotenuseadjacenthypotenuse = oppositeadjacent = tan θ

The hypotenuse divides out of the compound fraction, leaving exactly the opposite-over-adjacent ratio. Keep tan θ = sin θcos θ close at hand. It will keep coming back once these ideas reach beyond right triangles.

Worked Examples

To find a side, name the side you know and the side you want, relative to the angle you have, then pick the ratio that uses exactly those two. To find an angle from two sides, write the ratio they form and undo it with an inverse trigonometric function.

Example 2In a right triangle, an acute angle measures 32° and the hypotenuse is 10 units. Find the lengths of the two legs.
  1. Let the opposite leg be a and the adjacent leg be b. Opposite and hypotenuse means sine: sin 32° = a10.
  2. Solve: a = 10 sin 32° ≈ 10(0.5299) ≈ 5.30.
  3. Adjacent and hypotenuse means cosine: cos 32° = b10, so b = 10 cos 32° ≈ 10(0.8480) ≈ 8.48.
  4. Check with the Pythagorean theorem: 5.302 + 8.482 ≈ 28.09 + 71.91 = 100.00 = 102.
  5. Opposite leg ≈ 5.30 units; adjacent leg ≈ 8.48 units.
Example 3A right triangle has legs 7 and 24, with hypotenuse 25. Find both acute angles.
  1. Check that it is a right triangle: 72 + 242 = 49 + 576 = 625 = 252.
  2. Let θ be the angle opposite the side of length 7. Then sin θ = 725 = 0.28.
  3. Undo the sine: θ = sin−1(0.28) ≈ 16.26°.
  4. The two acute angles of a right triangle sum to 90°, so the other angle is 90° − 16.26° = 73.74°.
  5. Cross-check with tangent: tan θ = 724 ≈ 0.2917, and tan−1(0.2917) ≈ 16.26°, the same answer. The acute angles are about 16.26° and 73.74°.

On Your Calculator: Ratios In, Angles Out

The Desmos scientific calculator is a fast tool for single trig evaluations, and the graphing calculator below has the same keys. Try these three in order.

  1. Make sure the calculator is in degree mode. Type sin(32) and confirm you get about 0.5299, matching Example 2.
  2. Now go backward. Type sin−1(0.28), often found on the 2nd or shift function of the sine key, and confirm you recover 16.26° from Example 3.
  3. Try tan−1(7/24) directly, without simplifying the fraction first. Most calculators accept the division inside the function, and it returns the same 16.26°.
