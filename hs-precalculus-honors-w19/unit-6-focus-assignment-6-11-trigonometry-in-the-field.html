Where the idea comes fromIn 1792, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain set out to measure the distance from Dunkirk to Barcelona along the meridian through Paris, because the proposed metric system needed a meter that belonged to no king and no nation: one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. They spent seven years carrying that survey across a war-torn countryside, station by station, each leg a triangle solved with right-triangle trigonometry. One sloppy angle, uncaught, would have carried into every triangle after it. Their survey was not perfect: the Earth is not a perfect sphere, so the model they assumed was slightly off, not their mathematics. Even an imperfect model, honestly documented, can serve for generations. Carry your survey across one page, checked the same way.

Words you will use
Angle of elevation / depressionThe angle between a horizontal line of sight and a line of sight up to a higher object (elevation) or down to a lower one (depression). The two are equal by alternate interior angles whenever the sightlines connect the same two points.
Bearing / headingA direction given as an angle measured clockwise from north, from 0° to 360°. It is the convention navigators and surveyors use, and it differs from the standard-position angle, which is measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis, that is, from east.
Heading-to-standard conversionThe formula θstandard = 90° − heading, plus or minus 360° to land between 0° and 360°. It translates a compass heading into the angle the unit circle expects, because north sits at standard-position 90°, not 0°.
TraverseA surveyed path made of straight legs, each with its own distance and heading. Its total displacement comes from adding every leg’s east–west and north–south components separately.

Angles in Standard Position

From Angles in Degrees and Radians

An angle in standard position has its vertex at the origin and starts on the positive x-axis, which points east. Positive angles turn counterclockwise. The quadrants are: I from 0° to 90°, II from 90° to 180°, III from 180° to 270°, IV from 270° to 360°. Example: 150° is a little less than a half turn, so it lands in Quadrant II.

aIn which quadrant does an angle of 110° land in standard position?

2

Coterminal Angles

From Coterminal Angles, Arc Length, and Sectors

Two angles are coterminal when they end on the same ray. They differ by whole turns, so add or subtract 360° until the angle lands between 0° and 360°. Example: −160° + 360° = 200°. A negative angle is a clockwise turn.

bType the angle between 0° and 360° that is coterminal with −110°.

3

Sine, Cosine and Tangent in a Right Triangle

From Right Triangle Trigonometry

For an acute angle θ: sine is opposite over hypotenuse, cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse, tangent is opposite over adjacent (SOH-CAH-TOA). To find a side, name the side you know and the side you want, pick the ratio that links them, then solve. When the unknown is on top of the ratio, multiply; when it is on the bottom, divide. Keep the calculator in degree mode.

Example: from 20 m away, the angle of elevation to a roof is 30°. Then tan 30° = h20, so h = 20 tan 30° ≈ 11.5 m.

cFrom a point 60 m from a flagpole’s base, the angle of elevation to the top is 38°. Find the flagpole’s height to the nearest tenth of a meter.

4

Elevation, Depression and Two Stations

From Solving with Right Triangles

The angle of elevation is measured up from the horizontal; the angle of depression is measured down from it. They are equal for the same two points, because the horizontal lines are parallel (alternate interior angles). So a depression angle from a high point is also the angle at the bottom of the right triangle.

A two-station survey has two unknowns, a height and a distance, so one sighting is never enough. Each station gives one equation for the same height, and the two equations are solved together.

dFrom a hilltop, the angle of depression to a boat is 30°. What is the angle of elevation from the boat up to the hilltop?

Worked exampleA surveyor measures the angle of elevation to the top of a tower as 35°. She walks 40 m directly away and measures again: 20°. Find the tower’s height.
  1. Declare: let x be the distance from the first point to the base, and h the tower’s height.
  2. First point: tan 35° = hx. Second point, 40 m farther back: tan 20° = hx + 40.
  3. Both equations describe the same h, so x tan 35° = (x + 40) tan 20°. Collect the x terms: x(tan 35° − tan 20°) = 40 tan 20°.
  4. x(0.7002 − 0.3640) = 40(0.3640), so x(0.3362) = 14.559 and x ≈ 43.30 m.
  5. h = x tan 35° ≈ 43.30(0.7002) ≈ 30.32 m.
  6. Verify with the other station: (x + 40) tan 20° = 83.30(0.3640) ≈ 30.32 m, a match. The tower is about 30.3 m tall.
Worked exampleAn aircraft flies level at 3.2 km. The angle of depression to the nearer boat is 52°, and to the farther boat, 29°. Find the distance between the boats.
  1. The depression angle equals the elevation angle at each boat, so for each boat tan(angle) = altitudehorizontal distance.
  2. Solve for the distance: horizontal distance = altitudetan(angle).
  3. Near boat: dnear = 3.2tan 52° ≈ 3.21.2799 ≈ 2.500 km. Far boat: dfar = 3.2tan 29° ≈ 3.20.5543 ≈ 5.773 km.
  4. Subtract: 5.773 − 2.500 ≈ 3.27 km. The steeper angle belongs to the nearer boat. If your two distances came out reversed, check which is near and which is far.
5

Reference Angles and Signs Around the Circle

For an angle θ in standard position, the point where its ray meets the unit circle is (cos θ, sin θ). Cosine is the x-coordinate and sine is the y-coordinate. The reference angle is the acute angle between the ray and the x-axis, so the values match those of the reference angle up to a sign.

QIref = θcos +, sin +
QIIref = 180° − θcos −, sin +
QIIIref = θ − 180°cos −, sin −
QIVref = 360° − θcos +, sin −

This sign pattern is often remembered as ASTC: in Quadrant I All are positive, in II Sine, in III Tangent, in IV Cosine. Example: 140° is in Quadrant II, reference angle 180° − 140° = 40°, so cos 140° = −cos 40° (negative) and sin 140° = sin 40° (positive).

eType the reference angle of 300°.

fFor an angle of 200°, what are the signs of cosine and sine?

From a Compass Heading to East and North

A navigator’s heading is measured clockwise from north. The unit circle’s standard position is measured counterclockwise from east. The two do not line up by accident, so convert on purpose, every time:

θstandard = 90° − heading (add 360° if the result is negative)

After traveling distance d on that heading, the east component is d cos θstandard and the north component is d sin θstandard. A negative value means west or south, exactly as the signs of the quadrant dictate. Skip the conversion, and every sign and quadrant after it is wrong.

Worked exampleA ship holds a compass heading of 250° for 200 nautical miles. Find its eastward and northward displacement.
  1. Convert first. θstandard = 90° − 250° = −160°, which is coterminal with −160° + 360° = 200°.
  2. Locate and reduce. 200° is in Quadrant III, reference angle 200° − 180° = 20°. In Quadrant III cosine and sine are both negative.
  3. cos 200° = −cos 20° ≈ −0.9397 and sin 200° = −sin 20° ≈ −0.3420.
  4. East: 200(−0.9397) ≈ −187.9 nm. North: 200(−0.3420) ≈ −68.4 nm.
  5. The ship moves about 187.9 nm west and 68.4 nm south. The minus signs give direction, not error.
  6. Sanity check: a heading of 250° lies between due south (180°) and due west (270°), closer to west, and the components agree. A displacement that came out mostly northward would signal a conversion mistake before a single sign was checked.

gConvert a compass heading of 310° to a standard-position angle between 0° and 360°.

hThat standard angle, 140°, is in Quadrant II. For a boat on heading 310°, what are the signs of the east and north components?

Common mistakeFeeding a compass heading straight into cosine and sine. A heading and a standard angle start from different rays (north, east) and turn in opposite directions (clockwise, counterclockwise). Using one as the other gives numbers of the right size attached to the wrong directions, which is more dangerous than an obviously wrong answer because it looks plausible until someone checks it against a compass.
AI check, factualA student asked an AI tool for the east and north components of a 400 m displacement on heading 155°. It answered: east ≈ −362.5 m, north ≈ 169.0 m, “about 362.5 m west and 169.0 m north.” The arithmetic was flawless, but the tool put the heading straight into cosine and sine and skipped 90° − 155° = −65° ≡ 295°. A heading of 155° lies between due east and due south, so the displacement should be southeast. The correct values are about 169.0 m east and 362.5 m south: the right magnitudes, attached to the wrong directions. A tool can compute correctly and still skip a step, and it cannot see the field, so check any displacement, yours or a machine’s, against the compass. No tool is required for this project.

Adding Legs: Totals, Distance and the Bearing Home

To combine legs, add all the east components together and all the north components together. The straight-line distance is √(east total)2 + (north total)2. The compass bearing of the total displacement is the angle measured clockwise from north. The reciprocal bearing, the course that sails back, is that bearing plus or minus 180°.

Example: a total of 30 nm east and 40 nm north has distance √302 + 402 = 50 nm. Its bearing is about 37° (a little east of north), so the way back is about 217°.

jA boat ends 30 nm east and 40 nm north of where it started. How far is it, in a straight line, from its start?

kThe boat in that example is on a bearing of about 37° from its start. What bearing would sail it back?
