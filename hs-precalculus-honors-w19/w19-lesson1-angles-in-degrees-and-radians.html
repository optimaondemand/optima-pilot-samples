Angles in Degrees and Radians

Focus

A circle already carries a unit of length: its own radius. What happens when you measure an angle with it, and how do you move between that measure and degrees?

Objectives
  • Define a radian as the measure of a central angle whose arc is exactly as long as the circle’s radius.
  • Convert angle measures between degrees and radians, in both directions.
  • Explain why the radian, not the degree, is the unit that comes from the circle itself.
  • Estimate whether a radian measure is more or less than a given number of degrees without a calculator.
  • Say how the degree or radian mode of a calculator changes a trig answer.

A

Where the Degree Came From

Around 150 BC, the astronomer Hipparchus of Nicaea wanted a table that would give the length of any chord of a circle for any central angle. To build it he needed a fixed way to measure angles, and he took the one the Babylonian astronomers had handed down: divide the full circle into 360 parts. Nobody is certain why 360 won. It is close to the number of days in a year, and it is wonderfully divisible (by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12 and more), which matters a great deal when you work with no calculator. His chord table was the first trigonometric table in history, and it was built in degrees, a unit whose value is convenience, not necessity.

The radian was not fixed by anyone’s habit. It falls out of the circle itself. Roger Cotes described the idea as early as 1714, and the physicist James Thomson coined the name radian in 1873. A circle already has a natural unit of length built in, its radius, so why not measure an angle by how many radius-lengths of arc it cuts off?

Did you knowThe 360-degree circle and the splitting of a degree into 60 minutes and each minute into 60 seconds both trace back to Babylonian astronomers, who calculated in base 60 instead of base 10. The same inheritance gives us the 60 minutes in an hour and 60 seconds in a minute. A navigator reading a latitude of 40° 42′ 51″ is reading a number system built for the sky thousands of years ago.
B

The Definition of a Radian

Draw a circle of radius r around a point. Mark off an arc along the circle whose length is exactly r, one radius-length of arc. The central angle that arc cuts off measures 1 radian.

The circumference of the whole circle is 2πr, which is exactly 2π radius-lengths, so a full rotation measures exactly 2π radians. A full rotation is also 360°, so the two systems are tied together by one master equation:

2π radians = 360°

Divide by 2 and you get π radians = 180°, the single fact every conversion is built from. One radian is 180π ≈ 57.2958°, a little less than 60°. Lay a radius along the edge of the circle and mark where it ends: you land a bit short of one sixth of the way around, since 60° is exactly one sixth of a circle.

All the words in this lesson
AngleThe figure formed when a ray is rotated about its endpoint. The starting position of the ray is the initial side, its position after rotation is the terminal side, and the shared endpoint is the vertex.
Standard positionAn angle drawn with its vertex at the origin and its initial side along the positive x-axis. A positive angle rotates counterclockwise from there; a negative angle rotates clockwise.
DegreeA unit of angle measure made by dividing one full rotation into 360 equal parts. The number 360 is a human choice, inherited from Babylonian sky-watchers, not a necessity of geometry.
RadianThe measure of a central angle that cuts off (subtends) an arc exactly as long as the circle’s radius. Unlike the degree, it is not a convention: it comes directly from the circle’s geometry.

Worked Examples

Since π radians = 180°, every conversion multiplies by one fraction. To convert degrees → radians, multiply by π180. To convert radians → degrees, multiply by 180π. Always reduce the fraction: an unreduced radian measure is correct but unfinished.

Example 1Convert 135° and −45° to radians.
  1. Multiply by π180: 135° × π180 = 135π180.
  2. Reduce by dividing top and bottom by 45: 135π180 = 3π4.
  3. Do the same for the negative angle: −45° × π180 = −45π180 = −π4.
  4. 135° = 3π4 rad and −45° = −π4 rad.
Example 2Convert 5π6 radians to degrees.
  1. Multiply by 180π: 5π6 × 180π.
  2. The π in the numerator cancels the π in the denominator: 5 · 1806.
  3. Divide 180 by 6 first: 5 · 30 = 150.
  4. 5π6 rad = 150°. The π’s always cancel going from radians to degrees, which is why radian measures are usually left with a bare π in them instead of a decimal.
Example 3Convert 200° to radians. This one will not reduce to a familiar angle.
  1. Multiply by π180: 200° × π180 = 200π180.
  2. Divide top and bottom by 20: 10π9.
  3. 200° = 10π9 rad. Not every angle converts to a tidy multiple of π6 or π4, and the conversion factor works on any angle at all.

Degree Mode and Radian Mode

Every scientific or graphing calculator, including the Desmos scientific calculator, has a setting that decides how it reads every angle you type: degree mode or radian mode. It has no way of knowing which one you intend. A silent mode mismatch is the most common source of wrong answers in trigonometry, and the cause is usually not bad algebra.

In the Desmos graphing calculator, open the settings (wrench) icon and find the angle-mode toggle. Practice switching it, then type sin(30) in degree mode and again in radian mode. After solving a conversion on paper, you can check it the same way: evaluate the degree measure inside a trig function such as sin in degree mode, switch to radian mode, and evaluate your converted answer inside the same function. If the conversion is right, both modes return the same output.

Try the Desmos calculator · opens the graphing calculator
Common mistakeLeaving the calculator in the wrong mode. In radian mode, typing sin(30) to mean thirty degrees computes the sine of 30 radians instead. Subtracting multiples of 2π puts 30 radians in the fourth quadrant, so the result is about −0.988, not the 0.5 you wanted. It is a completely different number with the opposite sign.
