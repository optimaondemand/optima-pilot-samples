Solving with Right Triangles

A surveyor measures angles and one short distance, then finds heights no one can climb. How does a right triangle turn those into a height, and what does a small angle error cost?

A

Where Surveyors Started

In 1615, a Dutch astronomer named Willebrord Snellius set out to do what Eratosthenes had done nearly two thousand years earlier, measure the size of the Earth, but with a new technique. Instead of one angle and one long-distance measurement, Snellius built a chain of triangles across the Dutch countryside. He measured one short baseline precisely with rods, then measured only angles from each triangle’s vertices to the next, and let trigonometry compute every other distance in the chain without another tape measure.

This method, triangulation, became the standard tool for mapping nations for the next three centuries. A single measured baseline, extended honestly through hundreds of connected triangles, could place mountains, rivers and borders on a map with a precision that no chain of individual distance measurements could match, because angles, unlike ropes and chains stretched across rough ground, do not stretch, sag or wear out.

The three ratios sine, cosine and tangent now leave the geometry classroom and go into the field. Every problem here reduces to a right triangle you already know. The only new step is finding the horizontal line of sight and deciding whether you look up from it or down.

B

Elevation and Depression Are One Angle

An observer at point A looks up at an object at point B with an angle of elevation θ. An observer standing at B and looking down at A measures an angle of depression that is also θ. The two horizontal lines, one at A and one at B, are parallel, and the line of sight AB is a transversal crossing both. So the angle of elevation and the angle of depression are alternate interior angles, and alternate interior angles cut by a transversal across parallel lines are always congruent.

Draw the horizontal firstIn every problem the angle is measured from the horizontal, never from the vertical and never from the line of sight itself. Draw the horizontal line first, every time, before you draw anything else.
C

Worked Examples

Example 1Angle of elevation. A surveyor stands 200 feet from the base of a water tower and measures the angle of elevation to the top as 35°. Find the height of the tower.
  1. Draw the horizontal. The 200 ft lies along it, adjacent to the 35° angle. The tower’s height is opposite it.
  2. Opposite and adjacent call for tangent: tan 35° = h200.
  3. Solve for h: h = 200 tan 35° ≈ 200(0.7002) ≈ 140.04 ft.
  4. The tower is about 140.0 ft tall.
Example 2Angle of depression. A lighthouse keeper stands at the top of a lighthouse 60 meters above sea level and sights a boat at an angle of depression of 12°. Find the horizontal distance from the base of the lighthouse to the boat.
  1. Draw the horizontal line of sight at the keeper’s eye level. The angle of depression to the boat is 12°.
  2. The horizontal lines are parallel, so the angle of elevation from the boat up to the keeper is also 12°.
  3. In the triangle, the 60 m height is opposite that angle and the unknown distance d is adjacent: tan 12° = 60d.
  4. Solve: d = 60tan 12° ≈ 600.2126 ≈ 282.3 m.
  5. The boat is about 282.3 m from the base of the lighthouse.

Common mistakeMeasuring the angle from the wrong reference line. A student who treats a given angle as measured from the vertical will swap sine and cosine, or the two legs of the tangent ratio, throughout the whole problem. A

A Surveying Problem

Example 3Two stations, one height. A survey crew wants the height of a tower but cannot reach its base, because a river is in the way. From point A they measure the angle of elevation to the top as 25°. They walk 150 ft directly toward the tower to point B and measure 40°. Find the tower’s height and the distance from B to its base.
  1. Let h be the tower’s height and x the distance from B to the base. Then A is x + 150 from the base, and two right triangles share h.
  2. From B: tan 40° = hx, so h = x tan 40°.
  3. From A: tan 25° = hx + 150, so h = (x + 150) tan 25°.
  4. Both expressions equal h, so set them equal: x tan 40° = (x + 150) tan 25°.
  5. Collect the x terms and factor: x tan 40° − x tan 25° = 150 tan 25°, so x(tan 40° − tan 25°) = 150 tan 25°.
  6. Solve: x = 150 tan 25°tan 40° − tan 25° ≈ 150(0.4663)0.8391 − 0.4663 = 69.9450.3728 ≈ 187.6 ft.
  7. Find h from either equation: h = x tan 40° ≈ 187.6(0.8391) ≈ 157.4 ft.
  8. Check with the other equation: (x + 150) tan 25° ≈ (337.6)(0.4663) ≈ 157.4 ft ✓. Both triangles agree.
  9. The tower is about 157.4 ft tall, and B is about 187.6 ft from its base. No measurement touched the tower, which is exactly the situation a real crew faces when a river, a fence or private property blocks the way.
Two triangles, one shared sideWhenever two right triangles are built from two observation points to the same object, they share the object’s height or distance as a common side. Writing that shared quantity two ways, once from each triangle, turns two equations into one equation in one unknown. This is the core move of every two-station problem, and it needs only the tangent ratio.

A

What a Small Error Costs

An angle reading goes into the tangent ratio, and the ratio multiplies it by the distance. A reading that is off by a small amount does not stay small: it is carried through the multiplication into the height, and the farther the tower, the larger the effect. That is why careful measurement is a matter of responsibility and not only of procedure. A surveyor who reports a bare number as if it were certain has hidden how far it could be wrong.

Three practices guard against an error this size: repeat the reading, use a longer baseline, or state a margin of error alongside the reported number. The first two make the measurement better. The third makes the report honest about what is left.

Did you knowThe Great Trigonometric Survey of nineteenth-century India used the two-station method of Example 3, scaled up across enormous distances, to measure Himalayan peaks no one could climb with instruments. In the 1850s, a mathematician on the survey team named Radhanath Sikdar calculated from a network of distant angle readings that a peak then known as “Peak XV” was the tallest mountain on Earth, the peak later named Mount Everest. The story goes that the computed height came out to a suspiciously round 29,000 feet, and the survey published 29,002 feet instead, so that no one would mistake a genuine measurement for a careless guess. That is responsibility, recorded in a published number.
