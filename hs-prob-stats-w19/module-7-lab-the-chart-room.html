Why Build a Lie

A graph can tell the truth in every number and still mislead. A lie you make with your own hands is a lie you will spot on sight. A lie that is labeled is a lesson; an unlabeled one is a lie, so the page will not let you capture the dishonest chart without its label.

You will make your choice of lie, then argue with numbers which question catches it and how far it stretches the truth. The honest chart comes first, so you know exactly what the lie changed.

From Statistics Begins with Data: Individuals and Categories

Each thing a table describes is an individual, and each characteristic recorded about it is a variable. A variable that sorts individuals into named groups is categorical. Data invented for practice must say so: this lab calls its numbers constructed data.

Worked micro-example. In the garden harvest, every vegetable picked is an individual (20 of them), and its kind (tomato, pepper, bean, squash or herb) is one categorical variable.

1In the garden harvest, what is one individual?

B

From Frequency Tables: Counts and Relative Frequency

A frequency table lists each category once with its count. The counts must add to the total number of individuals, and that total check catches a skipped or doubled count. The relative frequency of a category is its count ÷ the total, and its percent is the relative frequency × 100.

Worked micro-example. Tomatoes: 6 ÷ 20 = 0.30, so 30%. The total check: 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 20.

From Displaying Categorical Data: Bars and Angles

A bar chart draws one bar per category, with equal widths and equal gaps, and its length shows the count. A circle graph divides a circle into slices, one per category. A slice’s angle is its relative frequency × 360°, and the angles of all the slices must add to 360°.

Worked micro-example. Tomatoes: 0.30 × 360 = 108, so the tomato slice is 108°.

4Beans have a relative frequency of 0.20. What is the angle of the bean slice, in degrees?

D

The Five Questions of an Honest Graph

A careful reader asks five questions of every graph.

  1. Zero start? For bars and pictographs, does the axis begin at zero?
  2. Full range? Is the whole time range shown, or only a convenient slice?
  3. Equal gaps? Are equal gaps on the axis equal intervals of the variable?
  4. Sizes true? Do the sizes of icons and bars match the values: length for bars, same-size icons for counts?
  5. Labels, source? Are the title, units, sample size, bin width and source given?

This lab uses two of them most. Zero start? catches bars whose bottoms have been cut off. Sizes true? catches bars or icons that grow faster than the counts they stand for.

Worked micro-example, cut-off axis. True bars of 8 and 4 have a true ratio of 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Hide the bottom 2 units of each bar and the visible lengths are 6 and 2, so the picture says 6 ÷ 2 = 3 times. The data did not change; the picture did.

Worked micro-example, inflated icon. Double a token’s width and height and its face area becomes 2 × 2 = 4 times as big. Double its depth as well and its volume is 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times as big, though the count only doubled.
