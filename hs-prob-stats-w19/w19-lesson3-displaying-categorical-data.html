Displaying Categorical Data

Focus

A

The Table Becomes a Picture

A table is exact, but a picture lets the eye compare at a glance. For categorical data, two displays do most of the work: the bar chart and the circle graph (also called a pie chart). Both start from a frequency table: the count in each category, the relative frequency (each count divided by the total, so 14 ÷ 40 = 0.35), and the percent (the relative frequency × 100).

B

The Rules of a Bar Chart

A bar chart draws one bar per category, with the bar’s length showing the frequency (or the relative frequency). The rules are few, and each one protects the reader.

  • Equal widths, equal gaps. Only the length carries information.
  • Start at zero. A bar’s length is supposed to be proportional to its value. If the axis starts above zero, a bar twice as long no longer means twice as much.
  • Label everything. A title saying what was measured and on whom, both axes with units, and every category named.
  • Order the bars on purpose. If the categories have a natural order (grade levels, months), keep it. If not, ordering from tallest to shortest makes comparisons easy.

Reading the finished chart takes seconds: soccer leads, basketball is a close second, and volleyball is last. A bar chart is the best tool when you want to compare categories, including when there are many of them.

D

The Rule and the Check

A circle graph divides a circle into slices, one per category, with each slice’s angle proportional to its share of the whole. Since a full circle is 360°, angle = relative frequency × 360°.

ExampleFind the angle for every sport in the favorite-sport table, then check the total. (Constructed data.)
  1. Soccer: 0.35 × 360 = 126°.
  2. Basketball: 0.275 × 360 = 99°.
  3. Swimming: 0.20 × 360 = 72°.
  4. Tennis: 0.125 × 360 = 45°.
  5. Volleyball: 0.05 × 360 = 18°.
  6. Check: 126 + 99 + 72 + 45 + 18 = 360. Every circle graph you draw has this check built in.

F

Angles with a Calculator

The circle-graph rule is one line in any calculator, such as the free Desmos scientific calculator. For soccer, type 0.35*360 and read 126. Or start from the frequency itself: 14/40*360 gives 126. For basketball, 11/40*360 gives 99. Add all five angles as a check: 126+99+72+45+18 gives 360. If your total misses 360, find the slip before drawing.

In a spreadsheet, the table’s percent column is the same idea: numbers in a column, with a total at the bottom. It is much easier to catch a bad total in a column of numbers than in a finished picture.

1Swimming is 8 out of 40. Type 8/40*360. What angle do you get?

2Volleyball is 2 out of 40. Type 2/40*360. What angle do you get?

3Type 126+99+72+45+18. What total do you get?

4The percent column in your spreadsheet adds to 99.5 instead of 100. What does that tell you?

G

Bar Chart or Circle Graph?

The same table can become either picture. Which one you choose depends on the job.

Bar chartThe tool for comparing categories, including when there are many of them.
Circle graphThe tool for one job: showing how a whole divides into a few parts. It only makes sense when the categories do not overlap and together make up the whole.

With more than about six slices, or slices close in size, the eye cannot judge angles well, and a bar chart wins.

A firm ruleNever use a circle graph when the percents do not total 100% because people could choose more than one answer.

Five Questions Before You Show a Chart
An AI-generated character and voice.

Honest-display checklist

  1. Does the title say what was measured and on whom?
  2. Are the axes labeled with units, and the categories named?
  3. Do the bars start at zero?
  4. Is the sample size n stated?
  5. If it is made up or a classroom survey, does it say so?
J

The Honest-Display Checklist

Keep this list. Before you show anyone a categorical display, ask: (1) Does the title say what was measured and on whom? (2) Are the axes labeled with units, and the categories named? (3) Do the bars start at zero? (4) Is the sample size n stated? (5) If it is made up or a classroom survey, does it say so? A display that fails these checks may still be pretty, but it is no longer honest.
