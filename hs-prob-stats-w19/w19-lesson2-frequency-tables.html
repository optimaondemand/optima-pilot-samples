Frequency Tables

Focus

Counting is the oldest statistical act, and dividing by the total is what makes counts comparable. What do you count, and what do you divide by?

Objectives
  • Build a frequency table from raw data with a tally and a total check.
  • Compute relative frequencies and percents, and verify that they add to 1 (or 100%, allowing for rounding).
  • Use a cumulative relative frequency column to answer “at most” questions for numerical data.
  • Choose relative frequency, not raw counts, to compare groups of different sizes.

A

From a Pile of Answers to a Table

Suppose you ask 25 students, “How do you usually get to school?” and write the answers in the order they arrive (constructed data):

Car, Bus, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike, Car, Bus, Bus, Walk, Car, Car, Bike, Bus, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bus, Car, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike

That is raw data: honest, complete, and nearly unreadable. The first move, the move John Graunt made with the Bills of Mortality, is to sort and count. The number of times a value occurs is its frequency. A frequency table lists each value or category beside its frequency.

B

Tally Without Losing Anyone

MethodTally the 25 answers (constructed data) without skipping or double-counting anyone.
  1. Counting by eye through twenty-five words invites a skipped or double-counted answer, so use the discipline of a careful clerk. List the categories once, down the left side.
  2. Go through the raw list one entry at a time, in order, and make one tally mark by the matching category as you cross the entry out.
  3. Count the marks to get each frequency:
    Way to schoolFrequency
    Car10
    Bus8
    Walk4
    Bike3
    Total25
  4. Check: the frequencies must add up to n, the number of individuals. Ten plus eight plus four plus three is 25, so every student is accounted for. If the total did not match, someone was lost or counted twice.
  5. That total check is not decoration. It is the habit that catches errors before they become conclusions.

D

Relative Frequency: What Fraction?

“Ten students take the car” is a fact about this class. “Two-fifths of students take the car” is a statement we can compare with other classes. The relative frequency of a value is its frequency divided by the total:

relative frequency = frequencyn

Multiply by 100 to express it as a percent.

ExampleFind the relative frequency and percent for each way of getting to school (constructed data, n = 25).
  1. For the car riders, divide the frequency by the total: 10 ÷ 25 = 0.40.
  2. Multiply by 100 for the percent: 0.40 × 100 = 40%.
  3. Do each category the same way:
    Way to schoolFreq.RelativePercent
    Car1010/25 = 0.4040%
    Bus88/25 = 0.3232%
    Walk44/25 = 0.1616%
    Bike33/25 = 0.1212%
    Total251.00100%
  4. The second check: relative frequencies add to 1 (percents to 100%). Here 0.40 + 0.32 + 0.16 + 0.12 = 1.00.
  5. Why must they? Together the categories contain every individual, and each individual is counted once. The pieces of the whole add to the whole.

F

When Rounding Spoils the Total

Not every division comes out clean. Suppose 30 students choose among four clubs with frequencies 5, 6, 8, and 11 (constructed data). Change how many decimal places you keep and watch the total.

ClubFrequencyExactPercent

Nothing is wrong. Each value was rounded, and rounding errors can pile up a tenth of a percent. The honest practice has two parts: round only at the end (keep exact fractions while you work), and if a published table totals 100.1% or 99.9%, say so in a note (“percents may not sum to 100 because of rounding”) instead of quietly nudging one entry. A total that is off by a visible mistake, such as 108%, means an error you must find.

1Percents to one decimal place for the four clubs: 16.7%, 20.0%, 26.7%, 36.7%. What do they add to? (Type the number only.)

G

A Numerical Variable and the Cumulative Column

Frequency tables work for numerical variables too, when the values repeat. Twenty students report how many books they read last month (constructed data). Each value is a row.

ExampleBuild the cumulative relative frequency column, then answer: what fraction of these students read at most 2 books?
  1. Divide each frequency by n = 20 to get the relative frequencies, which add to 1.00.
    Books readFreq.RelativeCumulative
    020.100.10
    150.250.35
    270.350.70
    340.200.90
    420.101.00
    Total201.00
  2. The cumulative relative frequency is a running total of the relative frequencies from the top: 0.10, then 0.10 + 0.25 = 0.35, then 0.35 + 0.35 = 0.70, and so on, ending at 1.00.
  3. Its job is to answer “at most” questions. Read the row for 2: 0.70, or 70%.
  4. Check by counting: 2 + 5 + 7 = 14 students, and 14 ÷ 20 = 0.70.
  5. Cumulative columns only make sense when the values have a natural order, so they belong to numerical data, not to categories like “car” and “bus.”

I

Why Relative Frequency Is the Honest Way to Compare

Section A has 20 students and 8 say they prefer taking a course online. Section B has 50 students and 15 say the same (constructed data). Which section is more in favor? By raw counts, Section B (15 beats 8). By relative frequency: Section A, 8 ÷ 20 = 40%; Section B, 15 ÷ 50 = 30%. Section A is the more favorable group, even though it contributed fewer students.

GroupSizeOnlineShare
Section A2088/20 = 40%
Section B501515/50 = 30%
HonestyWhen groups differ in size, comparing frequencies compares the sizes of the groups as much as their opinions. Relative frequencies put every group on the same footing, and reporting which denominator you used is part of telling the truth about a table.

Relative Frequencies in Desmos and Excel

You need a notebook and pencil, a scientific calculator such as Desmos, and a spreadsheet such as Excel (desktop or web).

In Desmos: type 10/25. Desmos answers with the exact fraction 2/5; click the fraction-to-decimal toggle to the left of the result to see 0.4. To get a percent, type 10/25*100 and you get 40. Check a whole table at once: 10/25+8/25+4/25+3/25 gives 1. Keep fractions exact until the last step, then round once.

In ExcelPut the categories in A2:A5 and the frequencies in B2:B5, with Frequency in B1 and Relative frequency in C1.
  1. Click cell B6 and type =SUM(B2:B5), then Enter. This is n.
  2. Click C2 and type =B2/SUM($B$2:$B$5), then Enter. The dollar signs lock the total in place so it does not slide when you copy the formula down.
  3. With C2 selected, drag the small square at its lower-right corner down to C5.
  4. Select C2:C5, go to the Home tab, and in the Number group click the Percent Style (%) button to display percents.
  5. In C6 type =SUM(C2:C5). You should see 100%. That is your second check, done by the machine.
