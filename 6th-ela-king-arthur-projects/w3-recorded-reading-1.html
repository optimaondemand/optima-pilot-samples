More tips: choosing well

Green’s dialogue is where most readers sound best, because two people wanting different things gives your voice something to do.

The two swords. Arthur pulls the sword because Kay needs one, not because he wants a crown. The moment he hands it over is short and worth reading slowly.

The Green Knight. Gawain kneels for the return blow. There is a lot of build-up and then very little happens very quickly, which makes it hard to read aloud well and satisfying when you do.

Beaumains. Sir Kay names him “Fair Hands” and puts him in the kitchen for a year. Lady Linnet tells him he stinks of the kitchen and should go home. He answers her courteously every time. Any of those exchanges reads well aloud because two people want different things in every line.

Pick a moment where somebody decides something, or where what a character says and what they actually mean come apart.

B · The disputed label
You are on a books podcast with two minutes. Argue something debatable about the passage you just read, and take the side most listeners will not.
Name the arguable thing in this passage: a choice a character makes, a line that can be read two ways, or something the author does that you think is a mistake.
Say your position in one sentence. If nobody could disagree with it, it is not an argument yet.
Point at the words. You just read them aloud, so quote them back.
Answer the strongest objection, the one someone who disagrees would come at you with.
C · One line recast
Rewind to one moment inside the passage you just read, and change it.
Name the exact sentence or moment you are changing.
Say what happens instead, in enough detail that a listener can picture it.
Say what the author’s version does that yours does not. Then say whether you still prefer yours, and why.
