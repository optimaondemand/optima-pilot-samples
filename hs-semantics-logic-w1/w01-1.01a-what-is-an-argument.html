What Is an Argument?

A statement supported by other statements, and how it differs from an assertion, an opinion, and an explanation.

You’ve probably done things like,

  • Decided to bring a jacket because it looked like rain.
  • Told someone they should study more because they failed the last test.
  • Figured your phone didn’t charge because the cord was damaged.

We do this all the time. So much so that what we are doing may not be clear to us. Sometimes a thing can be so familiar that we don't think about it, like our own shadows or gravity or the ground we walk on.

So let's find out what we are doing in those three examples above by contrasting them. What is present in those three cases that is not present when you say the following?

“I’m bringing a jacket.”

This statement expresses an intention, but it does not tell us why. Perhaps you will bring a jacket because you think it will rain, or because it is windy, or perhaps you just had an unfortunate incident with your spaghetti lunch that you want to cover up, or maybe your favorite baseball team is in the playoffs and you think it will bring them good luck. There are all sorts of reasons one might carry a jacket, and not all of them are good.

So, let's clarify the statement.

“I’m bringing a jacket because it looks like rain.”

Here we have two statements, "I'm bringing a jacket" and "it looks like rain." One of these statements is used to support the other.

What's important is that we can give more than a statement: we can give a reasoned conclusion. And that kind of thinking has a name. When we believe something because it follows from something else we believe, we’re making an argument.

A statement supported by another statement or set of statements is called an argument.

Definition · Argument

In logic, an argument is a group of statements where some (called premises) are intended to support another (called the conclusion).

Now let’s go deeper into what doesn’t count as an argument.

ArgumentClaim + reason

One statement is offered to support another.

AssertionClaim, no support

States something, but gives no reason for it.

OpinionA preference

Reports what someone likes or feels.

ExplanationWhy it happened

Gives the cause of a fact everyone already accepts.

Assertion

An assertion states something without support. Here is one example.

“Dogs are better pets than cats.”

Also an assertion, unless backed up by reasons. Now, here's an important point. Assertions are not wrong or mistaken simply because they are assertions. It may be the case that dogs make better pets than cats. But we would have to hear the argument first. In other words, we would have to see the statements brought forward to support the conclusion.

Here are some more examples.

"Beauty is in the eye of the beholder." "God does not exist." "Abortion is a human right."

These are each assertions. They are statements made here without any support. That does not make them wrong or mistaken. It does mean, however, that they are made without an attempt to rationally persuade. Now, a lot of people have done a lot of hard thinking to try and support these specific claims with evidence. But without that support, as they are presented here, they merely are assertions to which it is more than reasonable to respond, "prove it!"

Opinion

An opinion is a personal belief or preference. It’s about what someone feels rather than a reason to feel that way.

“I like scary movies.”

That’s a personal preference, not an argument. But let's imagine that you say,

“Scary movies are better because they’re more exciting.”

Is this opinion or argument? This is tricky because it sounds like mere opinion. The best genre of film is a matter of opinion, right? Well, maybe, and then again, maybe not. That's a question to be settled with arguments. In the second quote above, you’ve moved from opinion to argument, because you’re giving a reason. Now, it may not be a good reason. Are movies always better for being more exciting? That's a question for another time. The point here is that the giving of a reason can turn an expression of opinion into an argument, whether that argument is good or bad!

Explanation

This is the trickiest one to understand. Arguments are an attempt to persuade a listener to believe something. An explanation tells us why something happened, not why we should believe that it happened.

Let’s compare. Here are two examples, both of which involve the same fact: the ground is wet. Imagine a friend (call him Jim) saying this to you.

“The ground is wet because it rained last night.”

Now ask yourself, does Jim think I agree or disagree with the claim "the ground is wet"? Typically, explanations like these would be given only if everyone already agrees to the fact to be explained.

Now consider the following line of thought by Jim.

“It rained last night. So, the ground must be wet.”

Try to imagine the setting. Perhaps you two are on your way to a golf course, or maybe you are discussing plans to do some landscaping work on the lawn. It doesn't matter. The point is this. Does Jim think that you would agree or disagree with the statement "The ground is wet" if he didn't also tell you that it rained last night? Clearly not, because he is using the fact about last night's weather to support his belief that the ground is wet.

Key difference:

  • An explanation starts with a fact and gives the cause.
  • An argument starts with evidence and tries to support the fact.

In logic, an argument is a set of statements where one statement is supported by one or more other statements. The purpose of an argument is to show that a particular claim is true or reasonable to believe because it follows from the supporting reasons.

Arguments are often confused with other kinds of statements, but they are different in important ways. An assertion is just a claim: stating something without offering any support. An opinion is a personal preference or feeling, not something meant to be supported. An explanation tells why something happened, assuming we already agree that it did happen. In contrast, an argument tries to support that something is true by giving reasons for it.
