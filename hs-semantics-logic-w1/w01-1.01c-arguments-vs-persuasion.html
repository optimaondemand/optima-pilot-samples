Arguments vs. Persuasion

All arguments aim to persuade, but not all persuasion gives reasons.

Arguments help us persuade each other by supporting our beliefs. At first, it might seem like arguing and persuading are the same thing. After all, both try to get someone to believe something. But there’s a very important difference.

Imagine that Jim has been hypnotized to believe that it rained last night. He now confidently says, “It definitely rained.” Technically, he’s been persuaded (his belief has changed) but not through any reasoning. He didn’t think about evidence. He didn’t consider reasons. He just believes it because of psychological manipulation.

Now imagine a different case. Jim is unsure whether it rained, and someone says to him,

“The sidewalks are wet, and there are fresh puddles everywhere. So it probably rained last night.”

This time, Jim is also persuaded, but through reasons. He hears evidence and changes his belief based on it. This is rational persuasion. And that’s what argument is all about.

An argument is a way of persuading someone by giving them reasons, not by pressuring them, manipulating them, or distracting them.

So while all arguments aim to persuade, not all persuasion involves arguments. Some persuasion uses emotion, repetition, or authority. But logic is concerned only with rational persuasion: the kind that appeals to your mind, not your feelings.

Activity

Two ways to change a mind

Not all persuasion uses logic. Here are some common ways people are persuaded without being given reasons:

Emotional appeal

“Don’t you care about your children’s future?”

Popularity

“Everyone’s doing it. Don’t be left out.”

Authority

“A famous doctor said so, so it must be true.”

Repetition

“You’ve heard it a hundred times. It must be right.”

Fear

“If you don’t act now, terrible things will happen.”

Each of these might make someone feel convinced. After all, peer pressure, for example, is a very powerful motivator. But none of them answer the real question: Why should I believe this is true?

Only an argument tries to answer that.

Rational persuasion matters because it’s how we find our way to the truth.

Not all persuasion is equal. People can be persuaded by emotions, habits, peer pressure, repetition, or even fear. But none of those things are reliable guides to what’s actually true.

Reasons are.

When we’re persuaded by a good argument, we’re not just convinced; we’re convinced for a reason. And that reason can be examined, tested, and questioned. It can stand up to challenge or fall apart under scrutiny. That’s what makes rational persuasion different: it holds itself accountable to truth.

Think of it this way:

  • Feelings can change.
  • Trends can be wrong.
  • Authorities can lie.

But a sound argument, based on true premises and valid reasoning, leads us closer to something we can rely on.

That’s why logic doesn’t just care about whether someone was persuaded. It asks how and why they were persuaded. It values the quality of belief, not just the outcome.

Rational persuasion helps us believe things not because they feel good or sound convincing, but because they’re justified. That’s how we move from opinion to knowledge.

Comparing Persuasion and Argument

Let’s look at two versions of someone trying to convince you to vote for a candidate:

Mere persuasion

“You should vote for her because she’s just like us and she really cares.”

This might sound nice. It appeals to emotion and identity, but it doesn’t give a reason that can be examined. It may persuade, but it’s not a logical argument.

Argument

“You should vote for him because he has a strong record of improving schools and lowering crime in his district.”

This gives you evidence. It’s meant to justify the belief that he's the best candidate. That makes it a logical argument.

Not all persuasion is bad, but logic is concerned with a very specific kind: persuasion by reason.

That’s what makes arguments so powerful. They aim not just to convince, but to convince rightly: to help us believe things because we should believe them.
