Capstone Project & Course Final

The last week. Time to ship your capstone project, take the cumulative final, and look back across a year of digital discoveries. This week pulls together every skill from W1 through W35 — and points you forward to high school and beyond.

01
Section 1 · The Capstone Project

A working project that shows what you can do

The capstone is your year-end demonstration. Over the last five weeks (W31-W35) you've been building toward this — running a mini-SDLC, doing user research, pairing with a teammate, using Git, and debugging. Now you ship. The capstone is not the biggest project of the year — it's the most complete one. Working, tested, documented, and presented. Three principles guide the whole week:

📘 Vocabulary

Capstone project — a culminating project that demonstrates the skills built across a course.

README — a short text file that explains what your project is, how to install/run it, and how to use it. Universal convention in software.

Demo — a live or recorded walkthrough of your project, showing what it does for a real user.

02
Section 2 · Five Capstone Deliverables

What you submit by the due date — five pieces, all required

A complete capstone has five pieces. Each is small on its own. Together they show that you can build, document, demonstrate, reflect, and present — the full professional set:

💻1 · Working code
What: Your actual program — in a Git repository, with multiple commits showing your progress. Standard: small but complete. Finishing 100% of a small project beats 60% of an ambitious one.
📝2 · README
What: A short doc explaining the project. Sections: what it does, how to run it, how to use it, who it's for. About 1 page is right.
🎞️3 · Demo
What: A 2-3 minute video (or live walkthrough) showing the project working. Tip: demo from a USER's perspective, not from a code-walkthrough perspective.
🪞4 · Reflection
What: A 1-page written reflection. Topics: what worked, what didn't, what you'd do differently, what you learned about your own work.
📽️5 · Short presentation
What: 5-minute presentation to the class. Structure: the problem, your solution, what you learned, what's next. Slide deck in Google Slides or Canva.
✅ Check for Understanding

Submission self-check: Look at your project right now. Do you have all five deliverables? If you're missing any, that's the priority for this week. (Working code is necessary, but not enough — the other four make it a complete submission.)

03
Section 3 · Polishing vs. Shipping

The hardest call of the final week — when to stop tweaking and submit

Many students get stuck at 90% complete — endlessly polishing one feature while the README and demo go unmade. The submission deadline is real. A finished small project always beats an unfinished bigger one. Two mindsets compete:

⚠️ Endless Polish
"One more feature..."
  • Keeps adding features to avoid finishing
  • Working code, but no README, no demo, no reflection
  • Misses the submission deadline
  • Result: a near-finished project that nobody sees
✅ Ship
"Working + documented = done."
  • Locks scope EARLY in the week
  • Spends the last 2 days on README, demo, reflection
  • Submits on time, even if features are smaller than dreamed
  • Result: a complete submission everyone can review
💭 Think About It

This is the same lesson real engineers learn over their careers. The product that ships beats the product that's almost ready. Apple, Google, Microsoft — every major company has a stack of "almost ready" projects that were killed because they never shipped. Your capstone is a chance to practice the discipline: at some point, stop tweaking, write the README, record the demo, hit submit. The skill of finishing is rarer and more valuable than the skill of starting.

04
Section 4 · Capstone Submission Checklist

Three moves this week — Mon-Wed, Thu, Fri

Use this rhythm to land all five deliverables by the due date. Don't try to do everything at once — stagger by day:

1
Mon-Wed — Finish the code & commit to Git
Code done by the end of Day 3. Lock the feature list on Day 1 and don't add new ones. Multiple small commits with clear messages (W34). Debug systematically (W35).
2
Day 4 — Write the README & record the demo
README in the repo: what the project does, how to run it, who it's for, what's still planned. Demo: a 2-3 minute screen recording. Show, don't tell. Watch the demo yourself — does it make sense to someone who's never seen the project?
3
Day 5 — Reflection, presentation, submit
Write the 1-page reflection. Build the 5-slide presentation. Submit everything in Canvas. Then take the cumulative course final. End of the year. Celebrate — you finished an entire 8th-grade CS curriculum.
💡 Did You Know?

Every professional software project ends with the same kind of week you're having now — feature freeze, documentation push, demo, ship, retrospective. The discipline of the "ship week" is universal across the industry. Students who learn to navigate it in 8th grade are dramatically ahead of peers who only learn it on their first job. The skills are the same; the scale changes.

05
Section 5 · Four Traits of a Strong Capstone

What separates a complete submission from an excellent one

Four traits separate solid capstone submissions from outstanding ones. Aim for all four — and use them as a final self-check before you hit submit:

✅ Works End-to-End
A new user can actually USE the project from start to finish without it breaking. Test it yourself one more time before submitting.
📝 Clearly Documented
README reads like a real product page. A stranger could understand what it is and how to use it in 60 seconds.
🧠 Honest Reflection
The reflection talks about what DIDN'T work AND what you'd do differently. Honesty about gaps is more impressive than false perfection.
🔥 Polished Demo
A 2-3 minute video that shows the project working for a real user. Practiced, clear, and free of long awkward pauses.
🌎 Real-World Connection

The capstone is your first real software portfolio piece. Save the repo, the README, and the demo — these are the kind of artifacts you'll show at high-school CS clubs, summer programs, scholarship applications, and (eventually) internships. The discipline of capstone week — ship a complete, documented, demoed, reflected-upon piece of work — is exactly what employers and admissions teams look for.

You've finished an entire year of Digital Discoveries. Twelve weeks ago you were learning what variables and conditionals were; now you're shipping a working software project with a README and a demo. That arc is real. Be proud of it. And keep building.

README
A short text file explaining what a project is and how to use it. Universal convention in software.
Demo
A live or recorded walkthrough of a project, shown from the user's perspective.

An honest written look back at the work — what worked, what didn't, what you'd do next time.

To hand in the finished work. The discipline of stopping and submitting beats endless polishing.
Portfolio
A collection of your real, finished projects — used for clubs, applications, internships, and jobs.
Feature freeze
The moment when you stop adding new features and focus on finishing what's already started.
Retrospective
A look-back meeting (or written reflection) where teams discuss what went well and what to improve next time.
🪞 Year-End Reflection
Look back across the entire year of Digital Discoveries — and answer honestly.

This is the last reflection of the course. Write 10-15 sentences, more thoughtful than usual:

  • One topic from W1-W36 that genuinely changed how you think about technology. Be specific.
  • One skill you can do now that you absolutely couldn't do at the start of the year. (Coding? Debugging? Critical AI thinking? Design audit? Pick the most real one.)
  • One area where you'd like to get stronger before high school CS. Be honest — gaps named are gaps closeable.
  • One thing you'd build next — bigger than the capstone, that you'd actually be excited to attempt over the summer.
